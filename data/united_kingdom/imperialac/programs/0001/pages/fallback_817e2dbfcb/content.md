Humans are, of course, [animals](https://www.britannica.com/animal/animal)—more particularly, members of the order [Primates](https://www.britannica.com/animal/primate-mammal) in the subphylum [Vertebrata](https://www.britannica.com/animal/vertebrate) of the phylum Chordata. Like all [chordates](https://www.britannica.com/animal/chordate), the human [animal](https://www.britannica.com/animal/animal) has a bilaterally symmetrical body that is characterized at some point during its development by a dorsal supporting rod (the [notochord](https://www.britannica.com/science/notochord)), gill slits in the region of the [pharynx](https://www.britannica.com/science/pharynx), and a hollow dorsal [nerve](https://www.britannica.com/science/nerve-anatomy) cord. Of these features, the first two are present only during the embryonic stage in the human; the notochord is replaced by the vertebral column, and the pharyngeal gill slits are lost completely. The dorsal nerve cord is the spinal cord in humans; it remains throughout life.

[Characteristic](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/Characteristic) of the vertebrate form, the human body has an internal [skeleton](https://www.britannica.com/science/skeleton) that includes a backbone of vertebrae. Typical of mammalian structure, the human body shows such characteristics as [hair](https://www.britannica.com/science/hair-anatomy), [mammary glands](https://www.britannica.com/science/mammary-gland), and highly developed sense organs.

Beyond these similarities, however, lie some profound differences. Among the [mammals](https://www.britannica.com/animal/mammal), only humans have a predominantly two-legged ([bipedal](https://www.britannica.com/science/bipedalism)) posture, a fact that has greatly modified the general mammalian body plan. (Even the [kangaroo](https://www.britannica.com/animal/kangaroo), which hops on two legs when moving rapidly, walks on four legs and uses its tail as a “third leg” when standing.) Moreover, the human brain, particularly the neocortex, is far and away the most highly developed in the animal kingdom. As intelligent as are many other mammals—such as [chimpanzees](https://www.britannica.com/animal/chimpanzee) and [dolphins](https://www.britannica.com/animal/dolphin-mammal)—none have achieved the [intellectual](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/intellectual) status of the human species.

## Chemical composition of the body

Chemically, the human body consists mainly of [water](https://www.britannica.com/science/water) and of [organic compounds](https://www.britannica.com/science/organic-compound)—i.e., lipids, proteins, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids. Water is found in the extracellular fluids of the body (the [blood plasma](https://www.britannica.com/science/plasma-biology), the [lymph](https://www.britannica.com/science/lymph), and the interstitial fluid) and within the cells themselves. It serves as a solvent without which the chemistry of life could not take place. The human body is about 60 percent water by weight.

[Lipids](https://www.britannica.com/science/lipid)—chiefly [fats](https://www.britannica.com/topic/fat), [phospholipids](https://www.britannica.com/science/phospholipid), and [steroids](https://www.britannica.com/science/steroid)—are major structural components of the human body. Fats provide an energy reserve for the body, and [fat](https://www.britannica.com/topic/fat) pads also serve as insulation and shock absorbers. Phospholipids and the steroid [compound](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/compound) [cholesterol](https://www.britannica.com/science/cholesterol) are major components of the membrane that surrounds each cell.

[Proteins](https://www.britannica.com/science/protein) also serve as a major structural component of the body. Like lipids, proteins are an important [constituent](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constituent) of the [cell membrane](https://www.britannica.com/science/cell-membrane). In addition, such extracellular materials as hair and nails are composed of protein. So also is [collagen](https://www.britannica.com/science/collagen), the fibrous, elastic material that makes up much of the body’s skin, bones, tendons, and ligaments. Proteins also perform numerous functional roles in the body. Particularly important are cellular proteins called [enzymes](https://www.britannica.com/science/enzyme), which catalyze the chemical reactions necessary for life.

[Carbohydrates](https://www.britannica.com/science/carbohydrate) are present in the human body largely as fuels, either as simple [sugars](https://www.britannica.com/science/sugar-chemical-compound) circulating through the bloodstream or as [glycogen](https://www.britannica.com/science/glycogen), a storage compound found in the [liver](https://www.britannica.com/science/liver) and the muscles. Small amounts of carbohydrates also occur in cell membranes, but, in contrast to plants and many invertebrate animals, humans have little structural carbohydrate in their bodies.

[Nucleic acids](https://www.britannica.com/science/nucleic-acid) make up the genetic materials of the body. Deoxyribonucleic acid ([DNA](https://www.britannica.com/science/DNA)) carries the body’s hereditary master code, the instructions according to which each cell operates. It is DNA, passed from parents to offspring, that dictates the inherited characteristics of each individual human. Ribonucleic acid ([RNA](https://www.britannica.com/science/RNA)), of which there are several types, helps carry out the instructions encoded in the DNA.

Along with water and organic [compounds](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/compounds), the body’s constituents include various inorganic minerals. Chief among these are [calcium](https://www.britannica.com/science/calcium), [phosphorus](https://www.britannica.com/science/phosphorus-chemical-element), [sodium](https://www.britannica.com/science/sodium), [magnesium](https://www.britannica.com/science/magnesium), and [iron](https://www.britannica.com/science/iron-chemical-element). Calcium and phosphorus, combined as calcium-phosphate crystals, form a large part of the body’s bones. Calcium is also present as [ions](https://www.britannica.com/science/ion-physics) in the blood and interstitial [fluid](https://www.britannica.com/science/fluid-biology), as is sodium. Ions of phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium, on the other [hand](https://www.britannica.com/science/hand-anatomy), are abundant within the intercellular fluid. All of these ions play vital roles in the body’s metabolic processes. Iron is present mainly as part of [hemoglobin](https://www.britannica.com/science/hemoglobin), the oxygen-carrying pigment of the [red blood cells](https://www.britannica.com/science/red-blood-cell). Other mineral constituents of the body, found in minute but necessary concentrations, include [cobalt](https://www.britannica.com/science/cobalt-chemical-element), [copper](https://www.britannica.com/science/copper), [iodine](https://www.britannica.com/science/iodine), [manganese](https://www.britannica.com/science/manganese), and [zinc](https://www.britannica.com/science/zinc).

## Organization of the body

The [cell](https://www.britannica.com/science/cell-biology) is the basic living unit of the human body—indeed, of all organisms. The human body consists of trillions of cells, each capable of growth, [metabolism](https://www.britannica.com/science/metabolism), response to [stimuli](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/stimuli), and, with some exceptions, reproduction. Although there are some 200 different types of cells in the body, these can be grouped into four basic classes. These four basic cell types, together with their extracellular materials, form the fundamental tissues of the human body:

- nerve tissues, which conduct electrical impulses and make up the nervous system

[Bone](https://www.britannica.com/science/bone-anatomy) and [blood](https://www.britannica.com/science/blood-biochemistry) are considered specialized connective tissues, in which the intercellular matrix is, respectively, hard and liquid.

The next level of [organization](https://www.britannica.com/science/organ-biology) in the body is that of the [organ](https://www.britannica.com/science/organ-biology). An organ is a group of tissues that [constitutes](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitutes) a distinct structural and functional unit. Thus, the heart is an organ composed of all four tissues, whose function is to pump blood throughout the body. Of course, the heart does not function in isolation; it is part of a system composed of blood and blood vessels as well. The highest level of body organization, then, is that of the organ system.

The body includes nine major organ systems, each composed of various organs and tissues that work together as a functional unit. The chief constituents and prime functions of each system are:

- The musculoskeletal system (also referred to separately as the[muscle system](https://www.britannica.com/science/human-muscle-system)and the[skeletal system](https://www.britannica.com/science/human-skeleton)), composed of the skeletal muscles and bones (with about 206 of the latter in adults), moves the body and protectively houses its internal organs.
- The[respiratory system](https://www.britannica.com/science/human-respiratory-system), composed of the[breathing](https://www.britannica.com/science/breathing)passages, lungs, and muscles of[respiration](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/respiration), obtains from the air the[oxygen](https://www.britannica.com/science/oxygen)necessary for cellular metabolism; it also returns to the air the[carbon dioxide](https://www.britannica.com/science/carbon-dioxide)that forms as a waste product of such metabolism.
- The[circulatory system](https://www.britannica.com/science/human-cardiovascular-system), composed of the heart, blood, and blood vessels, circulates a transport fluid throughout the body, providing the cells with a steady supply of oxygen and nutrients and carrying away waste products such as carbon dioxide and toxic[nitrogen](https://www.britannica.com/science/nitrogen)compounds.
- The[digestive system](https://www.britannica.com/science/human-digestive-system), composed of the mouth,[esophagus](https://www.britannica.com/science/esophagus), stomach, and intestines, breaks down food into usable substances (nutrients), which are then absorbed from the blood or lymph; this system also eliminates the unusable or excess portion of the food as fecal matter.
- The[nervous system](https://www.britannica.com/science/human-nervous-system), composed of the sensory organs, brain, spinal cord, and nerves, transmits,[integrates](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/integrates), and analyzes sensory information and carries impulses to effect the appropriate muscular or glandular responses.
