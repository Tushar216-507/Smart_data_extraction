Religion in India is characterised by a diversity of religious beliefs and practices. Throughout India's history, [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion) has been an important part of the country's culture and the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent) is the birthplace of four of the [world's major religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_religious_groups), namely [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism), [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism), [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism), and [Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism), which are collectively known as native [Indian religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_religions) or [Dharmic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_religions) and represent approx. 83% of the total population of India.

India has the largest number of followers of Hinduism, Sikhism, Zoroastrianism, Jainism, and the [Bahá'í Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baháʼí_Faith_in_India) in the world. It further hosts the third most followers of Islam, behind [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia) and [Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan), and the ninth largest population of Buddhists.

The [Preamble to the Constitution of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preamble_to_the_Constitution_of_India) states that India is a [secular state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular_state), [and the](#cite_note-3) [Constitution of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_India) has declared the right to [freedom of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_religion) to be a [fundamental right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_Rights_in_India).

According to the 2011 census, 79.8% of the [population of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_India) follows [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism_in_India), 14.2% [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_India), 2.3% [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_India), 1.7% [Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism_in_India), 0.7% [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_in_India) and 0.4% [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism_in_India). [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism_in_India), [Sanamahism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanamahism) and [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism_in_India) also have an ancient history in India, and each has several thousands of Indian adherents. India has the largest population of people adhering to both Zoroastrianism (i.e. [Parsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsi) and [Iranis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irani_(India))) and the [Bahá'í Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baháʼí_Faith_in_India) in the world; [these religions are otherwise largely exclusive to their native](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESmith200894-5) [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran) where they originated from. Several tribal religions are also present in India, such as [Donyi-Polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donyi-Polo), [Sanamahism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanamahism), [Sarnaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarnaism), [Niamtre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niamtre), and others.

The [Constitution of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_India) declares India to be a [secular state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular_state) with no state religion. [The](#cite_note-legislative.gov.in-6) [Forty-second Amendment of the Constitution of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forty-second_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_India) enacted in 1976 asserts the [Preamble to the Constitution of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preamble_to_the_Constitution_of_India) as [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular). [The](#cite_note-42amend-7) [Supreme Court of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_India) in the 1994 case [S. R. Bommai v. Union of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S._R._Bommai_v._Union_of_India) established that India had been [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular) since the formation of the Republic on 26 January 1950. [Secularism in India is understood to mean not a complete separation of religion from state, but a state that participates in a neutral manner in the affairs of all religious groups and as well as](#cite_note-auto-8) [atheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism) while maintaining equidistance from all.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

However, the original copy of the Indian constitution has an illustration of [Rama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Ram), [Sita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sita), and [Lakshmana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakshman) in Part III on Fundamental Rights and [Rama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Rama) has been considered as the true guardian of people's rights. [Article 48 of the Indian constitution prohibits the slaughter of](#cite_note-9) [cows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle_in_religion_and_mythology) or calves (a sacred animal in [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism)) and the same is a criminal offence in most states of India.

Secularism is defined as a [basic structure doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_structure_doctrine) of the constitution through the argument of [Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kesavananda_Bharati_v._State_of_Kerala) case, that cannot be removed or amended by any means. [However, there is no mention of the term “Basic Structure” anywhere in the Constitution of India. The idea that the Parliament cannot introduce laws that would amend the basic structure of the constitution have been evolved judicially over time and many cases.](#cite_note-12)

The particular provisions regarding secularism and [freedom of religion in India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_religion_in_India) in the constitution are:

- Article 14: grants equality before the law and equal protection of the laws to all.
- Article 15: enlarges the concept of secularism to the widest possible extent by prohibiting discrimination on grounds of religion, race, [caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste_system_in_India), sex or place of birth.
- Article 25: Freedom of conscience and free profession, practice and propagation of any religion.
- Article 26: Freedom to manage religious affairs.
- Article 27: Freedom as to payment of taxes for promotion of any particular religion.
- Article 28: Freedom as to attendance at religious instruction or religious worship in certain educational institutions.
- Articles 29 and 30: provide cultural and educational rights to the minorities.
- Article 51A: i.e. Fundamental Duties obliges all the citizens to promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood and to value and preserve the heritage of the country's composite diverse culture.

Evidence attesting to [prehistoric religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric_religion) in the Indian "subcontinent" derives from scattered [Mesolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesolithic) rock paintings depicting dances and rituals.[Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) pastoralists inhabiting the [Indus Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley) buried their dead in a manner suggestive of spiritual practices that incorporated notions of an afterlife. [Other](#cite_note-Heehs_2002_39-24) [South Asian Stone Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asian_Stone_Age) sites, such as the [Bhimbetka rock shelters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhimbetka_rock_shelters) in central [Madhya Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhya_Pradesh) and the [Kupgal petroglyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kupgal_petroglyphs) of eastern Karnataka, contain rock art portraying religious rites and evidence of possible ritualised music.

The [Harappan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harappa) people of the [Indus Valley Civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation), which lasted from 3300 to 1400 BCE and was centered on the [Indus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_River) and [Ghaggar-Hakra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghaggar-Hakra_River) river valleys, may have worshiped an important [mother goddess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mother_goddess) symbolising fertility. [Excavations of Indus Valley Civilisation sites show seals with animals and "fire‑altars", indicating rituals associated with fire.](#cite_note-hvn-26) [A](#cite_note-Harappan_surprise-27) [Shivlinga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingam) of a type similar to that which is now worshiped by Hindus has also been found, [however this interpretation has been disputed by Srinivasan.](#cite_note-hvn-26)

Hinduism is often regarded as the oldest surviving religion in the world, [with roots tracing back to](#cite_note-29) [prehistoric times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistory), over 5,000 years ago. [Hinduism spread through](#cite_note-30) [parts of Southeastern Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism_in_Southeast_Asia), China, and Afghanistan. Hindus worship a single divine entity (paramatma, lit."first-soul") with different forms.

Hinduism's origins include the cultural elements of the Indus Valley Civilisation along with other Indian civilisations. [The oldest surviving text of Hinduism is the](#cite_note-32) [Rigveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda), produced during the [Vedic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_period) and dating to 1700–1100 BCE.[[γ]](#cnote_γ) [During the Epic and](#cite_note-Oberlies_1998_155-33) [Puranic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purana) periods, the earliest versions of the epic poems, in their current form including [Ramayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramayana) and [Mahabharata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata) were written roughly from 500 to 100 BCE, [although these were orally transmitted through families for centuries prior to this period.](#cite_note-34)

After 200 BCE, several schools of thought were formally codified in the [Indian philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_philosophy), including [Samkhya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samkhya), [Yoga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga), [Nyaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyaya), [Vaisheshika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaisheshika), [Purva-Mimamsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purva-Mimamsa), and [Vedanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedanta).

The [Śramaṇa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Śramaṇa) tradition includes [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism), [known endonymically as Jain Dharm, and](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEZimmer1953182–183-37) [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) [known endonymically as Bauddh Dharm, and others such as the](#cite_note-38) [Ājīvikas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ājīvika), [Ajñanas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajñana), and others.

The historical roots of Jainism in India have been traced to the 9th century BCE with the rise of [Parshvanatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parshvanatha), the 23rd [Tirthankar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirthankar), and his [Jain philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_philosophy), [and to](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEZimmer1953182–183-37) [Mahavira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahavira) (599–527 BCE), the 24th Jain Tirthankara. Jainism traces its roots further back to the first Tirthankara, [Rishabhanatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishabhanatha). Mahavira stressed on the five vows.

[Gautama Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha), who founded Buddhism, was born to the [Shakya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakya) clan just before [Magadha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magadha_(Mahajanapada)) (which lasted from 546 to 324 BCE) rose to power.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)] His family was native to the plains of [Lumbini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumbini), in what is now southern [Nepal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nepal). [Indian Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_in_India) peaked during the reign of [Ashoka the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashoka_the_Great) of the [Mauryan Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauryan_Empire), who patronised Buddhism following his conversion and unified the Indian subcontinent in the 3rd century BCE. [He sent missionaries abroad, allowing Buddhism to spread across Asia.](#cite_note-Ancient_History_Encyclopedia-42)[Indian Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_in_India) declined following the loss of royal patronage offered by the [Kushan Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushan_Empire) and such kingdoms as [Magadha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magadha_(Mahajanapada)) and [Kosala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosala).

The decline of Buddhism in India has been attributed to a variety of factors, which include the resurgence of Hinduism in the 10th and 11th centuries under [Sankaracharya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sankaracharya), the later Turkish invasion, the Buddhist focus on [renunciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nekkhamma) as opposed to familial values and private property, Hinduism's own use and appropriation of Buddhist and Jain ideals of renunciation and [ahimsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahimsa), and others. Although Buddhism virtually disappeared from mainstream India by the 11th century CE, its presence remained and manifested itself through other movements such as the [Bhakti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti) tradition, Vaishnavism, and the [Bauls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baul) of Bengal, who are influenced by the Sahajjyana form of Buddhism that was popular in Bengal during the [Pala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pala_Empire) period.

During the 14th–17th centuries, when North India was under Muslim rule, the [Bhakti movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti_movement) swept through Central and Northern India. The Bhakti movement actually started in the eighth century in south India (present-day [Tamil Nadu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_Nadu) and [Kerala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala)), and gradually spread northwards. [It was initiated by a loosely associated group of teachers or saints.](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESchomerMcLeod19871-44) [Dnyaneshwar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dnyaneshwar), [Chaitanya Mahaprabhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaitanya_Mahaprabhu), [Vallabhacharya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vallabhacharya), [Surdas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surdas), [Meera Bai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meera_Bai), [Kabir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabir), [Tulsidas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsidas), [Ravidas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravidas), [Namdeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namdeo), [Eknath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eknath), [Ramdas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samarth_Ramdas), [Tukaram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tukaram), and other mystics were some of the saints in the North. They taught that people could cast aside the heavy burdens of ritual and caste and the subtle complexities of philosophy, and simply express their overwhelming love for God. This period was also characterised by an abundance of devotional literature in vernacular prose and poetry in the ethnic languages of the various Indian states or provinces. The Bhakti movement gave rise to several different movements throughout India

During the Bhakti movement, many Hindu groups regarded as outside the traditional Hindu caste system followed Bhakti traditions by worshipping/following saints belonging to their respective communities. For example, Guru Ravidas was a [Chamar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamar) of Uttar Pradesh; Guru [Parsuram Ramnami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramnami_Samaj) was a [Chura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuhra)[[dubious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Accuracy_dispute#Disputed_statement)– [discuss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talk:Religion_in_India#Dubious)] of Chhattisgarh, and Maharishi Ram Naval was a [Bhangi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhangi) of Rajasthan. In their lifetimes, several of these saints even went to the extent of fighting conversion from foreign missionaries, encouraging only Hinduism within their communities. In Assam for example, tribals were led by Gurudev Kalicharan Bramha of the Brahmo Samaj; in Nagaland by Kacha Naga; and in Central India by Birsa Munda, Hanuman Aaron, Jatra Bhagat, and Budhu Bhagat.

The Kabir Panth is a religious movement based on the teachings of the Indian poet saint [Kabir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabir) (1398–1518).

Kabir sermonised a monotheism that appealed clearly to the poor and convinced them of their access to god with no liaison. He denied both Hinduism and Islam, as well as meaningless religious rituals, and condemned double standards. [This infuriated the orthodox aristocracy. No one could frighten Kabir who was bold enough to stand up for himself and his beliefs.](#cite_note-Kabir_in_His_Time,_And_Ours-46)

The Kabir Panth considers Kabir as its principal guru or even as a divinity—truth incarnate. Kabir's influence is testimony to his massive authority, even for those whose beliefs and practices he condemned so unsparingly. For Sikhs he is a forerunner and converser of Nanak, the originating Sikh Guru (spiritual guide). Muslims place him in Sufi (mystical) lineages, and for Hindus he becomes a Vaishnavite with universalist leanings.

[Guru Nanak Dev Ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Nanak_Dev_Ji) (1469–1539) was the founder of [Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism), known endonymically as Sikh Dharm. [The](#cite_note-Opinderjit_Kaur_Takhar_2016_147-50) [Guru Granth Sahib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Granth_Sahib) was first compiled by the fifth Sikh guru, [Guru Arjan Dev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Arjan_Dev), from the writings of the first five Sikh gurus and others saints who preached the concept of universal brotherhood, including those of the Hindu and Muslim faith. Before the death of [Guru Gobind Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Gobind_Singh), the Guru Granth Sahib was declared the eternal guru.[Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism) recognises all humans as equal before [Waheguru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waheguru), [regardless of colour, caste, or lineage.](#cite_note-52) [Sikhism](#cite_note-53) [strongly rejects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prohibitions_in_Sikhism) the beliefs of [fasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasting) ([vrata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vrata)), [superstitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_superstitions_in_India), [idol worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idolatry), [and](#cite_note-Service_2015-55) [circumcision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcision). [The Sikhs believe in one eternal god and follow the teachings of the](#cite_note-Glausiusz_2004-57) [10 gurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10_gurus), the [5 K's of Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5_K's_of_Sikhism), the hukums of [Guru Gobind Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Gobind_Singh), [Sikh Rehat Maryada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikh_Rehat_Maryada), and [Nitnem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitnem).

Jews first arrived as traders from [Judea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea) in the city of [Kochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kochi), Kerala, in 562BCE. [More Jews came as exiles from Jerusalem in the year 70CE, after the destruction of the](#cite_note-58) [Second Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Temple).

Christianity was believed to be introduced to India by [Thomas the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Apostle) (a direct disciple of [Jesus Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus)), [who visited](#cite_note-60) [Muziris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muziris) in Kerala in 52CE and [proselytised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proselytise) natives, who are known as [Saint Thomas Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Thomas_Christians) (also known as Syrian Christians or Nasrani) today. India's oldest church, the world's oldest existing church structure is believed to be built by Thomas the Apostle in 57CE, called [Thiruvithamcode Arappally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiruvithamcode_Arappally) or Thomaiyar or Kovil as named by the then [Chera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chera_Dynasty) king Udayancheral, is located at [Thiruvithancode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiruvithancode) in [Kanyakumari (Cape Comorin)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanyakumari_(Cape_Comorin)) district of [Tamil Nadu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_Nadu), it was declared an international St Thomas Pilgrim Centre. [There is a general](#cite_note-61) [scholarly consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholarly_consensus) that Christianity was rooted in India by the 6th century CE, including some communities who used [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language) liturgically. [Christianity in India consists of various denominations such as the](#cite_note-64) [Assyrian Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_East_in_India), [Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_India), [Protestantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism), [Oriental Orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodoxy_in_India) and others.

Most Christians live in South India, particularly in [Kerala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala), [Tamil Nadu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_Nadu), [Goa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goa), [and the average number of Christians is at 3.45% in areas such as](#cite_note-MIR-66) [Bombay (Mumbai)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombay_Metropolitan_Area). [There are also large Christian populations in](#cite_note-67) [Northeast India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeast_India).[Catholic Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Christian) was spread in the 16th century, particularly by the [Portuguese in Goa and Bombay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_in_Goa_and_Bombay). [Protestantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism_in_India) was introduced by [Protestant missionaries in the 18th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Protestant_missionaries_in_India).

Islam came to India in the early 7th century through the Arab traders in [Malabar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malabar_Coast) coast. It started to become a major religion during the Muslim rule in the Indian subcontinent. [The](#cite_note-Islam_and_Muslims_in_India-70) [Cheraman Juma Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheraman_Juma_Mosque) is the first mosque in India located in Methala, Kodungallur Taluk, Thrissur District in Kerala. [A legend claims that it was built in 629CE, which makes it the oldest mosque in the Indian subcontinent which is still in use.](#cite_note-Cheraman_Juma_Masjid-71) [The growth of Islam in India mostly took place under the](#cite_note-Cheraman_Juma_Masjid-71) [Delhi Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi_Sultanate) (1206–1526) and the [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire) (1526–1858), greatly aided by the mystic [Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism) tradition. [The growth of Islam was regionally uneven, and Islam had the greatest appeal to those who were not, or only superficially integrated into the Brahmanical fold, as in the case of Bengal.](#cite_note-73)

Currently, Islam is the second largest religion in India, with 14.2% of the country's population or roughly 172 million people identifying as adherents of Islam ([2011 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Census_of_India)). [The estimated number of Muslims in India for 2024 is projected to be around 204,760,392.](#cite_note-censusIndia2011-75)

It makes India the country with the largest Muslim population outside [Muslim-majority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_by_country) countries

[Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism) is an ancient religion with the largest religious grouping in India, with around 966million adherents as of 2011, composing 79.8% of the population. [Hinduism is diverse, with](#cite_note-census_2011_religion-84) [monotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism), [henotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henotheism), [polytheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism), [panentheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panentheism), [pantheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheism), [monism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monism), [atheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism_in_Hinduism), [animism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animism), [agnosticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnosticism), and [gnosticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnosticism) being represented. [The term Hindu, originally a geographical description, derives from the](#cite_note-95) [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit), Sindhu (the historical appellation for the Indus River), and refers to a person from the land of the river Sindhu. [Hindus following the traditional religion call it Sanatana Dharma (or "Eternal Way").](#cite_note-96) [The adherents of Sanatana Dharma call themselves as "Sanatani", the original word for the adherents of Sanatana Dharma.](#cite_note-97)

[Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam) is a [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism) religion centered on the belief in one God and following the example of [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad); it is the largest minority religion in India. About 14.2% of the country's population or approximately 172.2 million people identify as adherents of Islam (2011 census). [It makes India the country with the largest Muslim population outside](#cite_note-100) [Muslim-majority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_by_country) countries, and third among all countries. [Muslims are a majority in states](#cite_note-101) [Jammu and Kashmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jammu_and_Kashmir_(union_territory)) and [Lakshadweep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakshadweep), [and live in high concentrations in](#cite_note-web123-102) [Uttar Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uttar_Pradesh), [Bihar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bihar), [West Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Bengal), [Assam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assam), and [Kerala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala). [There has been no particular census conducted in India with regards to sects, but sources suggest the largest denomination is](#cite_note-COI_2001-103) [Sunni Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam), [with substantial populations of](#cite_note-state.gov-104) [Shia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Islam) and [Ahmadiyya Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmadiyya). Indian sources like [Times of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Times_of_India) and [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_News_&_Analysis) reported the Indian Shia Muslims population in mid-2005–2006 to be between 25% and 31% of entire Muslim population of India, which accounts them in numbers between 40 and 50 million.

[Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity) is a [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism) religion centred on the life and teachings of [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus) as presented in the [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament). It is the third largest religion of India, making up 2.3% of the population. [St. Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Apostle) is credited with introduction of Christianity in India. He arrived on the [Malabar Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malabar_Coast) in 52CE.[The tradition of origin among](#cite_note-nasrani-110) [Saint Thomas Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Thomas_Christians) relates to the arrival of [Saint Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Apostle), one of the 12 disciples of [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus) at the ancient seaport [Muziris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muziris) on the Kerala coast in 52CE. The families Sankaramangalam, Pakalomattam, Kalli, and Kaliyankal were considered particularly preeminent, and historically the most aristocratic [Syriac Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Christian) families tended to claim descent from these families.

It is also possible for [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic)-speaking [Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew) from [Galilee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilee) to make a trip to Kerala in the 1st century. The [Cochin Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochin_Jews) are known to have existed in Kerala around that time. The earliest known source connecting the apostle to India is the [Acts of Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_Thomas), likely written in the early 3rd century, perhaps in [Edessa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edessa).

[Marth Mariam Syro-Malabar Catholic Forane Church, Arakuzha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marth_Mariam_Syro-Malabar_Catholic_Forane_Church,_Arakuzha) was founded in 999.

The text describes Thomas' adventures in bringing Christianity to India, a tradition later expanded upon in early Indian sources such as the "Thomma Parvam" ("Song of Thomas"). Generally he is described as arriving in or around [Maliankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maliankara) and founding Seven Churches and half churches, or Ezharapallikal: Kodungallur, Kollam, [Niranam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niranam), [Nilackal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilackal) (Chayal), Kokkamangalam, [Kottakkavu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kottakkavu), [Palayoor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palayoor), Thiruvithamcode Arappalli and [Aruvithura church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._George's_Syro-Malabar_Catholic_Forane_Church,_Aruvithura) (half church). A number of 3rd- and 4th-century Roman writers also mention Thomas' trip to India, including [Ambrose of Milan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambrose_of_Milan), [Gregory of Nazianzus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_of_Nazianzus), [Jerome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome), and [Ephrem the Syrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephrem_the_Syrian), while [Eusebius of Caesarea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusebius_of_Caesarea) records that his teacher [Pantaenus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantaenus) visited a Christian community in India in the 2nd century. There came into existence a Christian community who were mainly merchants.

Christianity expanded in the rest of India during the period of British colonial rule. Christians constitute the majority of natives of [Nagaland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagaland) and [Mizoram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mizoram) as well as of [Meghalaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meghalaya) and have significant populations in [Manipur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manipur), [Goa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goa), [Kerala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala) and Mumbai.

[Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism) is a [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheistic) religion that began in fifteenth-century [Punjab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjab) with the teachings of [Guru Nanak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Nanak_Dev) and nine successive Sikh [gurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikh_Gurus). As of 2011, there were 20.8 million [Sikhs in India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism_in_India). [Punjab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjab,_India) is the spiritual home of Sikhs, and is the only state in India where Sikhs form a majority. There are also significant populations of Sikhs in neighbouring [Chandigarh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandigarh), [Himachal Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himachal_Pradesh), [Jammu division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jammu_division) of [Jammu and Kashmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jammu_and_Kashmir_(union_territory)), [Delhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi), and [Haryana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haryana). These areas were historically a part of [Greater Punjab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Punjab). However, there is no data for specific number of Nanak followers ([Nanakpanthis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanakpanthi)) in India, but they are believed to be in crores somewhere around 14 crores. [Karnail Singh Panjoli, member, Shiromani Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee, says that there are several communities within the term ‘Nanakpanthis’ too. “There are groups like Sikhligarh, Vanjaarey, Nirmaley, Lubaney, Johri, Satnamiye, Udaasiyas etc. who call themselves](#cite_note-113) [Nanakpanthis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanakpanthi). They follow guru Nanak and Sri Guru Granth Sahib.

[Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) is an Indian, [transtheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transtheism) religion and philosophy. Around 8.5million Buddhists live in India, about 0.7% of the total population. [Buddhism as a religion is practised mainly in the foothills of the](#cite_note-Religion-116) [Himalayas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himalayas) and is a significant religion in [Sikkim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikkim), [Arunachal Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arunachal_Pradesh), [Ladakh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ladakh), [Darjeeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darjeeling) in [West Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Bengal), and the Lahaul and [Spiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiti) districts of [Himachal Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himachal_Pradesh). Besides, a significant number of Buddhists reside in [Maharashtra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maharashtra). They are the Buddhists or [Navayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navayana) Buddhists who, under the influence of [B. R. Ambedkar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._R._Ambedkar) embraced Buddhism in order to escape the casteist practices within Hinduism. Ambedkar is a crucial figure, along with [Anagarika Dharmapala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anagarika_Dharmapala) of [Sri Lanka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka) and [Kripasaran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kripasaran) Mahasthavira of [Chittagong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chittagong) behind the revival of Buddhism in India in the 19th and 20th centuries. The escape of the 14th [Dalai Lama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalai_Lama), [Tenzing Gyatso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenzing_Gyatso) to India fleeing Chinese occupation of [Tibet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibet) in 1959 and the setting up of the [Tibetan Government in Exile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_Government_in_Exile) at [Dharamshala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharamshala) in [Mcleodganj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mcleodganj) in [Himachal Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himachal_Pradesh) has also accelerated the resurgence of Buddhism in India. The effective religion in [Sikkim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikkim), which joined the Indian Union in 1975 (making it India's 22nd state) remains [Vajrayana Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vajrayana_Buddhism), and [Padmasambhava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padmasambhava) or Guru Ugyen is a revered presence there.

[Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism) is a non-theistic Indian religion and philosophical system originating in [Iron Age India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age_India). Jains compose 0.4% (around 4.45 million) of India's population, and are concentrated in the states of [Gujarat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gujarat), [Karnataka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karnataka), [Madhya Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhya_Pradesh), [Maharashtra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maharashtra), and [Rajasthan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajasthan).

Also present in India, [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism) is a [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheistic) religion from the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant). There is today a very small community of Indian Jews. There were more Jews in India historically, including the [Cochin Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochin_Jews) of [Kerala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala), the [Bene Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bene_Israel) of [Maharashtra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maharashtra), and the [Baghdadi Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdadi_Jews) near [Mumbai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mumbai). Since Indian independence, two primarily proselyte Indian Jewish communities have developed in India: the [Bnei Menashe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bnei_Menashe) of [Mizoram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mizoram) and [Manipur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manipur), and the [Bene Ephraim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bene_Ephraim), also called Telugu Jews. Of the approximately 95,000 Jews of Indian extraction, fewer than 20,000 remain in India. Some parts of India are especially popular with Israelis, swelling local Jewish populations seasonally.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

As of the census of 2001, [Parsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsi_people) (followers of [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism) in India) represent approximately 0.006% of the total population of India, [with relatively high concentrations in and around the city of](#cite_note-117) [Mumbai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mumbai). Parsis number around 61,000 in India.

There are several tribal religions in India, such as [Donyi-Polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donyi-Polo). It is difficult to establish the exact numbers of [Baháʼís in India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baháʼí_Faith_in_India). Beginning in 1961, large numbers from scheduled castes became Baháʼís, and by 1993 Baháʼís reported about 2.2 million members, [though later sources have claimed 2million,](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESmith200894-5) [or "more than 1 million".](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMomen2008154–5-120)

[Sanamahism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanamahism) is the indigenous religion of [Meitei community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meitei_people) in [Manipur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manipur) which they follow along with [Vaishnavism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaishnavism).

Around 2.9 million people in India did not state their religion in the 2011 census and were counted in the category, "religion not stated". They were 0.24% of India's population. Their number have significantly increased 4 times from 0.7million in 2001 census at an average annual rate of 15%.[K. Veeramani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._Veeramani), a [Dravidar Kazhagam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dravidar_Kazhagam) leader, said that he believed that the number of atheists in India was actually higher as many people don't reveal their atheism out of fear.

According to the 2012 WIN-Gallup Global Index of Religion and Atheism report, 81% of Indians were religious, 13% were non-religious, 3% were convinced atheists, and 3% were unsure or did not respond.

The preamble to the [Constitution of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_India) proclaims India a "sovereign socialist secular democratic republic". The word secular was inserted into the Preamble by the [Forty-second Amendment Act of 1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forty-second_Amendment_Act_of_1976). It mandates equal treatment and tolerance of all religions. India does not have an official state religion; it enshrines the right to practice, preach, and propagate any religion. No religious instruction is imparted in government-supported schools. In [S. R. Bommai v. Union of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S._R._Bommai_v._Union_of_India), the [Supreme Court of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_India) held that secularism was an integral tenet of the Constitution and that there was separation of state and religion.

[Freedom of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_Rights_in_India#Right_to_freedom_of_religion) is a [fundamental right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_right) according to the Indian Constitution. The Constitution also suggests a [uniform civil code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_civil_code) for its citizens as a [Directive Principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directive_Principles_in_India). [This has not been implemented until now as Directive Principles are Constitutionally unenforceable. The Supreme Court has further held that the enactment of a uniform civil code all at once may be counter-productive to the unity of the nation, and only a gradual progressive change should be brought about (Pannalal Bansilal v State of Andhra Pradesh, 1996).](#cite_note-127) [In Maharishi Avadesh v Union of India (1994) the Supreme Court dismissed a petition seeking a writ of](#cite_note-hinduiyer-128) [mandamus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandamus) against the government to introduce a common civil code, and thus laid the responsibility of its introduction on the [legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislature_of_India).

Major religious communities not based in India continue to be governed by their own personal laws. While Muslims, Christians, Zoroastrians, and Jews have personal laws exclusive to themselves; Hindus, Jains, Buddhists, and Sikhs are governed by a single personal law known as [Hindu personal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Hindu_law). Article 25 (2)(b) of the Constitution of India states that references to Hindus include "persons professing the Sikh, Jain, or Buddhist religion". [Furthermore, the](#cite_note-Bakshi1996-130) [Hindu Marriage Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_Marriage_Act), 1955 defines the legal status of Jains, Buddhists, and Sikhs as legal Hindus but not "Hindus by religion". [Supreme Court in 2005 gave verdict that Jains, Sikhs, and Buddhist are part of broader Hindu fold, as they are Indic religions and interconnected to each other, though they are distinct religions.](#cite_note-Diwan1981-131)

Religion plays a major role in the Indian way of life. [Rituals, worship, and other religious activities are very prominent in an individual's daily life; it is also a principal organiser of social life. The degree of religiosity varies among individuals; in recent decades, religious orthodoxy and observances have become less common in Indian society, particularly among young urban-dwellers.[](#cite_note-PRC_2002-133)[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

The vast majority of Indians engage in religious rituals daily. [Most Hindus observe religious rituals at home.](#cite_note-religiouslife-134) [Observation of rituals vary greatly among regions, villages, and individuals. Devout Hindus perform daily chores such as worshiping](#cite_note-locceremonies-135) [puja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puja_(Hinduism)), fire sacrifice called [Yajna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna)[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)] at the dawn after bathing (usually at a family shrine, and typically includes lighting a lamp and offering foods before the images of deities), recitation from religious scripts like [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas), and [Puranas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puranas) singing hymns in praise of gods.

A notable feature in religious ritual is the division between purity and pollution. Religious acts presuppose some degree of impurity, or defilement for the practitioner, which must be overcome or neutralised, before or during ritual procedures. Purification, usually with water, is thus a typical feature of most religious action. [Other characteristics include a belief in the efficacy of sacrifice and concept of merit, gained through the performance of charity or good works, that will accumulate over time and reduce sufferings in the next world.](#cite_note-locceremonies-135)

Muslims offer [five daily prayers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salat#The_five_daily_prayers) at specific times of the day, indicated by [adhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adhan) (call to prayer) from the local mosques. Before offering prayers, they must ritually clean themselves by performing [wudu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wudu), which involves washing parts of the body that are generally exposed to dirt or dust. A recent study by the [Sachar Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sachar_Committee) found that 3–4% of Muslim children study in [madrasas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrasa) (Islamic schools).

Dietary habits in India are significantly influenced by religion. According to a survey, 31% of Indian population claims to be [vegetarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetarian), and mainly practice [lacto-vegetarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lacto-vegetarianism). [Vegetarianism is less common among](#cite_note-139) [Sikhs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diet_in_Sikhism), Muslims, Christians, [Bahá'ís](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baháʼí_Faith), [Parsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsis), and Jews.Despite the majority of population having no objection to meat consumption, globally India has the lowest meat consumption per capita. [Non-vegetarian Indians mostly prefer](#cite_note-140) [poultry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poultry), fish, other [seafood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seafood), goat, and sheep as their sources of meat. [Hinduism forbids beef while Islam forbids pork. The smaller populations of Christians, tribals, and some dalit communities have no objection to eating either beef or pork.](#cite_note-141)[Jainism requires followers, from all its sects and traditions, to be vegetarian. Furthermore, the religion also forbids](#cite_note-142) [Jains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_vegetarianism) from eating any vegetable that involves digging it from the ground. This rule, therefore, excludes all [root vegetables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_vegetable) such as potatoes, sweet potatoes, ginger, carrots, garlic, radishes, etc. from a Jain diet.

Occasions like birth, [marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_wedding), and death involve what are often elaborate sets of religious customs. In Hinduism, major life-cycle rituals include [annaprashan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annaprashan) (a baby's first intake of solid food), [upanayanam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanayanam) ("sacred thread ceremony" undergone by boys belonging to some upper-castes such as Brahmin and Kshatriya only), and [shraadh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shraadh) (paying homage to a deceased individual). [According to the findings of a 1995 national research paper, for most people in India, a](#cite_note-shraddha-144) [betrothal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arranged_marriage) of a young couple placing an expectation upon an exact date and time of a future wedding was a matter decided by the parents in consultation with [astrologers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrologer). [A significant reduction in the proportion of arranged marriages has however taken place since 1995, reflecting an incremental change.[](#cite_note-loclifecycle-143)[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

Muslims practice a series of life-cycle rituals that differ from those of Hindus, Jains, and Buddhists. [Several rituals mark the first days of life—including the whispering call to prayer, first bath, and shaving of the head. Religious instruction begins early. Male](#cite_note-locislamtrad-145) [circumcision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcision) usually takes place after birth; in some families, it may be delayed until after the onset of puberty.

Marriage requires a payment by the husband to the wife, called Meher, and the solemnisation of a marital contract in a social gathering. [After the burial of the dead, friends and relatives gather to console the bereaved, read and recite the Quran, and pray for the soul of the deceased.](#cite_note-locislamtrad-145) [Indian Islam is distinguished by the emphasis it places on shrines commemorating great Sufi saints.](#cite_note-locislamtrad-145)

Many Hindu families have their own family patron deity or the [kuladevata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuladevata). [This deity is common to a lineage or a clan of several families who are connected to each other through a common ancestor.](#cite_note-148) [The](#cite_note-150) [Khandoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khandoba) of Jejuri is an example of a Kuladevata of some Maharashtrian families; he is a common Kuladevata to several castes ranging from Brahmins to [Dalits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalit). [The practice of worshipping local or territorial deities as Kuladevata began in the period of the](#cite_note-151) [Yadava dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yadava_dynasty). [Other family deities of the people of Maharashtra are](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWalunjkar2009285–287-152) [Bhavani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhavani) of [Tuljapur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuljapur), [Mahalaxmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahalakshmi_Temple,_Kolhapur) of [Kolhapur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolhapur), [Renuka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renuka) of [Mahur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahur,_Maharashtra), and [Balaji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venkateswara) of Tirupati.

India hosts numerous pilgrimage sites belonging to many religions. Hindus worldwide recognise several Indian holy cities, including [Allahabad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allahabad) (officially known as Prayagraj), [Haridwar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haridwar), [Varanasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varanasi), [Ujjain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ujjain), [Rameshwaram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rameshwaram), and [Vrindavan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vrindavan). Notable temple cities include [Puri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puri_(city)), which hosts a major [Jagannath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagannath) temple and [Rath Yatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rath_Yatra) celebration; [Tirumala - Tirupati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirumala_-_Tirupati), home to the [Tirumala Venkateswara Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirumala_Venkateswara_Temple); and [Katra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katra,_Jammu_and_Kashmir), home to the [Vaishno Devi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaishno_Devi) temple.

[Badrinath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badrinath), [Puri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puri_(city)), [Dwarka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarka), and [Rameswaram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rameswaram) compose the main pilgrimage circuit of [Char Dham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Char_Dham) (four abodes) hosting the four holiest [Hindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu) temples: [Badrinath Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badrinath_Temple), [Jagannath Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagannath_Temple,_Puri), [Dwarkadheesh Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarkadheesh_temple,_Gujrat) and [Ramanathaswamy Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanathaswamy_Temple), respectively. The [Himalayan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himalaya) towns of [Badrinath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badrinath), [Kedarnath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kedarnath), [Gangotri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gangotri), and [Yamunotri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamunotri) compose the smaller [Chota Char Dham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chota_Char_Dham) (mini four abodes) pilgrimage circuit. The [Kumbh Mela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumbh_Mela) (the "pitcher festival") is one of the holiest of Hindu pilgrimages that is held every four years; the location is rotated among Allahabad (Prayagraj), Haridwar, [Nashik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashik), and [Ujjain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ujjain). The [Thalaimaippathi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swamithope_pathi) at [Swamithope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swamithope) is the leading pilgrim center for the [Ayyavazhis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayyavazhi).

Seven of the [Eight Great Places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_pilgrimage#The_Eight_Great_Places) of Buddhism are in India. [Bodh Gaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodh_Gaya), [Sarnath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarnath), and [Kushinagar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushinagar) are the places where important events in the life of Gautama Buddha took place. [Sanchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanchi) hosts a Buddhist [stupa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stupa) erected by the emperor [Ashoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashoka). Many Buddhist monasteries dot the Himalayan foothills of India, where [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) remains a major presence. These include the [Rumtek Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumtek_Monastery), [Enchey Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enchey_Monastery), and [Pemayangtse Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pemayangtse_Monastery) in [Sikkim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikkim), the [Tawang Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tawang_Monastery) in [Arunachal Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arunachal_Pradesh), the [Kye Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kye_Monastery) and [Tabo Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabo_Monastery) in [Spiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiti), the [Ghum Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghum_Monastery) in [Darjeeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darjeeling), and [Durpin Dara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durpin_Dara) Monastery in Kalimpong, the [Thikse Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thikse_Monastery) in [Leh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leh), the [Namgyal Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namgyal_Monastery) in [Dharamshala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharamsala), among many others.

For Sunni Muslims, the Dargah Shareef of Khwaza [Moinuddin Chishti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moinuddin_Chishti) in [Ajmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajmer) is a major pilgrimage site. [Other Islamic pilgrimages include those to the Tomb of Sheikh](#cite_note-153) [Salim Chishti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salim_Chishti) in [Fatehpur Sikri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatehpur_Sikri), [Jama Masjid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jama_Masjid,_Delhi) in Delhi, and to [Haji Ali Dargah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haji_Ali_Dargah) in Mumbai. [Dilwara Temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilwara_Temples) in [Mount Abu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Abu), [Palitana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palitana), [Pavapuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pawapuri), [Girnar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girnar), and [Shravanabelagola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shravanabelagola) are notable pilgrimage sites ([tirtha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirtha_(Jainism))) in Jainism.

The [Harmandir Sahib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmandir_Sahib) in [Amritsar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amritsar) is the most sacred [gurdwara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurdwara) of Sikhism.

Relatively new pilgrimage sites include the [samadhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samadhi) of [Meher Baba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meher_Baba) in [Meherabad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meherabad), which is visited by his followers from around the world [and the Saibaba temple in](#cite_note-155) [Shirdi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirdi).

Hinduism contains many different sub-cultures just like most other religions. The major aspects outlined above hold true for the majority of the Hindu population, but not all. Just as each state is home to an individual language, Hinduism harbours various sub-cultures whose traditions may or may not be shared by other Indians. A sect from Gujarat called the Prajapatis for example, holds water as the sacred ornament to every meal. Before and after a meal, an individual is expected to pour water in the palms of their right hand and sip the water three times. [This is often seen as a purification gesture: food is regarded as being holy and every individual must purify themselves before touching their food.](#cite_note-ReferenceA-157)

Other minor sects in India carry no specific name, but they are uniquely identified by the last names of each family. This convention is used more frequently in South India than in North India. For example, a relatively prominent sect in southern India prohibits making important decisions, commencing new tasks, and doing other intellectually or spiritually engaged actions after sunset. Historians believe that this tradition was derived from the concept of [Rahukaalam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahukaalam), in which Hindus believe that a specific period of the day is inauspicious. Stringent family beliefs are thought to have led to the development of a more constrained religious hierarchy. [Over time, this belief was extended to discourage taking major actions and even staying awake for long periods after sunset. Examples of families which follow this tradition include Gudivada, Padalapalli, Pantham, and](#cite_note-158) [Kashyap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashyap_(caste)).

India has a population of 123 crore (1,230,000,000) per a 2012 demographic survey by Indian government.

[Cambridge University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Press) in a 2004 demographic study found that there are 102.87 million [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheists) and [agnostics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnostics) living in India, thus constituting 9.1% of the total population, out of total 1.1296 billion people respectively. [According to the 2012 WIN-Gallup Global Index of Religion and Atheism report, 81% of Indians were religious, 13% were non-religious, 3% were convinced atheists, and 3% were unsure or did not respond.](#cite_note-160)

Survey evidence indicates that younger Indians display lower levels of religiosity than older youth cohorts. According to the [CSDS–Lokniti Youth Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_for_the_Study_of_Developing_Societies) (2017), 40% of youth overall fall into the category of low, very low, or no religiosity. Among the youngest respondents (ages 15–17 and 18–21), this proportion rises to 44%, suggesting that non-religiosity is more prevalent at younger ages. As age increases, the share of respondents reporting low or no religiosity declines gradually, falling to 37% among those aged 26–29 and 35% among those aged 30–34.

The [CSDS–Lokniti Youth Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_for_the_Study_of_Developing_Societies) (2017) also reveals substantial variation in religiosity across religious communities. Among [Indian Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_India), 38% of youth report high or very high religiosity, while only 29% report low or no religiosity. [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_India) youth similarly show relatively high religious commitment, with 47% reporting high religiosity and 36% reporting low or no religiosity. Sikh youth display a more balanced distribution, with 36% reporting high religiosity and 40% reporting low or no religiosity. Within Hindu youth, the data indicate significant internal diversity. Hindu upper-caste youth report the highest levels of religiosity within the Hindu population (41% high religiosity), while Hindu [OBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Other_Backward_Class) youth report 35%, Hindu [Scheduled Caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheduled_Castes_and_Scheduled_Tribes) youth 32%, and Hindu [Scheduled Tribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheduled_Castes_and_Scheduled_Tribes) youth 29%. Correspondingly, the proportion reporting low or no religiosity increases from 37% among Hindu upper castes to 47% among Hindu Scheduled Tribes, highlighting variation in religious intensity across caste groups within Hinduism.

Religious politics, particularly that expressed by the [Hindutva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindutva) movement, has strongly influenced [Indian politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_India) in the last quarter of the 20th century. Many of the elements underlying India's [casteism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casteism) and [communalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communalism_(South_Asia)) originated during the colonial era, when the colonial government frequently politicised religion in an attempt to stave off increasing [nationalistic sentiments in India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_nationalism). [The](#cite_note-162) [Indian Councils Act 1909](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Councils_Act_1909) (widely known as the Morley-Minto Reforms Act), which established separate Hindu and Muslim electorates for the Imperial Legislature and provincial councils, was particularly divisive, increasing tensions between the two communities.

Due to the high degree of oppression faced by the lower castes, the Constitution of India included provisions for [affirmative action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affirmative_action) for certain sections of Indian society. Many states ruled by the [Bharatiya Janata Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharatiya_Janata_Party) (BJP) introduced laws that made conversion more difficult; they assert that such conversions are often forced or allured. [The BJP, a national political party, also gained widespread media attention after its leaders associated themselves with the](#cite_note-bjpdalit-164) [Ram Janmabhoomi movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayodhya_debate) and other prominent religious issues.

A well-known accusation that Indian political parties make for their rivals is that they play vote bank politics, meaning give political support to issues for the sole purpose of gaining the votes of members of a particular community. Both the [Congress Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_National_Congress) and the BJP have been accused of exploiting the people by indulging in vote bank politics. The [Shah Bano case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shah_Bano_case), a divorce lawsuit, generated much controversy when the Congress was accused of appeasing the Muslim orthodoxy by bringing in a parliamentary amendment to negate the [Supreme Court's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_India) decision. After the [2002 Gujarat violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Gujarat_violence), there were allegations of political parties indulging in vote bank politics. [All the political parties in India are known to grant money to the State](#cite_note-166) [Waqf Boards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Waqf_Council) to appease a particular community.

[Caste-based politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste_politics_in_India) is also important in India; caste-based discrimination and the [reservation system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reservation_in_India) continue to be major issues that are hotly debated.

[Communalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communalism_(South_Asia)) has played a key role in shaping the religious history of modern India. After [Indian independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Independence_Act_1947) in 1947, India was [partitioned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_India) along religious lines into two states—the Muslim-majority [Dominion of Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion_of_Pakistan) (comprising what is now the [Islamic Republic of Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan) and the [People's Republic of Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh)) and the Hindu-majority [Union of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Republic_of_India) (later the [Republic of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India)). The partition led to rioting among Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs in Punjab, Bengal, Delhi, and other parts of India; 500,000 died as a result of the violence. The twelve million refugees that moved between the newly founded nations of India and Pakistan composed one of the largest mass migrations in modern history.[[Δ]](#cnote_Δ) [Since its independence, India has periodically witnessed large-scale violence sparked by underlying tensions between sections of its majority Hindu and minority Muslim communities. The Republic of India is secular; the](#cite_note-Symonds_1950_74-172) [Indian government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_India) recognises no official religion.

Communal conflicts have periodically plagued India since it became independent in 1947. [The roots of such strife lie largely in the underlying tensions between sections of its majority Hindu and minority Muslim communities, which emerged under the Raj and during the bloody](#cite_note-175) [Partition of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_India). Such conflict also stems from the competing ideologies of [Hindu nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_nationalism) versus [Islamic fundamentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_fundamentalism); both are prevalent in parts of the Hindu and Muslim populations. This issue has plagued India since before independence. The lack of education among the masses and the ease with which corrupt politicians can take advantage of the same has been attributed as the major reason for religious conflicts in India. Even though Freedom of religion is an integral part of the India constitution, the inability to hold a communal mob accountable for its collective actions has limited the exercise of religious freedom in India.

Alongside other major Indian independence leaders, [Mahatma Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahatma_Gandhi) and his Shanti sainiks ("peace soldiers") worked to quell early outbreaks of religious conflict in [Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal), including riots in [Calcutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolkata) (now in [West Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Bengal)) and [Noakhali District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noakhali_District) (in modern-day Bangladesh) that accompanied [Muhammad Ali Jinnah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_Jinnah)'s [Direct Action Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_Action_Day), which was launched on 16 August 1946. These conflicts, waged largely with rocks and knives and accompanied by widespread looting and arson, were crude affairs. Explosives and firearms, which are rarely found in India, were far less likely to be used.

Major post-independence communal conflicts include the [1984 Anti-Sikh riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Anti-Sikh_riots), which followed [Operation Blue Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Blue_Star) by the [Indian Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Army); heavy artillery, tanks, and helicopters were employed against the [Sikh partisans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khalistan_movement) inside the Harmandir Sahib, causing heavy damage to Sikhism's holiest [Gurdwara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurdwara). According to the Indian government estimates, the assault caused the deaths of up to 100 soldiers, 250 militants, and hundreds of civilians.

This triggered [Indira Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indira_Gandhi)'s assassination by her outraged Sikh bodyguards on 31 October 1984, which set off a four-day period during which Sikhs were massacred; The [Government of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_India) reported 2,700 Sikh deaths however human rights organisations and newspapers report the death toll to be 10,000–17,000. In the aftermath of the riot, the [Government of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_India) reported 20,000 had fled the city, however the [PUCL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PUCL) reported "at least" 50,000 displaced persons.

The most affected regions were neighbourhoods in [Delhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi). Human rights organisations and the newspapers believe the massacre was organised. [The collusion of political officials in the massacres and the failure to prosecute any killers alienated normal](#cite_note-Watch/Asia(U.S.)1994-179) [Sikhs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhs) and increased support for the [Khalistan movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khalistan_movement). The [Akal Takht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akal_Takht), the governing religious body of [Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism), considers the killings to be a [genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genocide).

Other incidents include the 1992 [Bombay riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombay_riots) that followed the demolition of the [Babri Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babri_Mosque) as a result of the [Ayodhya debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayodhya_debate), and the [2002 Gujarat violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Gujarat_violence) where 790 Muslims and 254 Hindus were killed and which was preceded by the [Godhra Train Burning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godhra_Train_Burning). [Lesser incidents plague many towns and villages; the representative was the killing of five people in](#cite_note-HRW_2006_265-181) [Mau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mau,_Uttar_Pradesh), Uttar Pradesh, during Hindu–Muslim rioting, which was triggered by the proposed celebration of a Hindu festival.
