# crime

- What is a crime?

  A crime is an intentional act that is usually considered socially harmful or dangerous. It is specifically defined, prohibited, and punishable under

  [criminal law](https://www.britannica.com/topic/crime-law/Introduction#ref1). Most countries have a criminal code outlining criminal laws.

  Essential elements of a crime include a voluntary action or failure to act, and a certain state of mind. Criminal law classifies crimes for assigning cases to different courts. Social changes often lead to new criminal laws and the obsolescence of older ones.

- What is the purpose of law in society?

  The purpose of law in society is to deter or punish serious wrongdoing or to compensate the victims of such activity. Criminal law addresses acts construed as offenses against the public, society, or the state, such as murder, assault, theft, and drunken driving. The traditional approach to criminal law views crime as morally wrong, with criminal sanctions serving to make offenders atone for harm and expiate moral guilt. Modern views consider the main purpose of criminal law to be the prevention of crime. Courts exist to impartially judge whether a person has committed a crime and, if so, what type of punishment should be administered.

- How can laws help prevent crime?

  Laws can help prevent crime through various mechanisms. Deterrence theory suggests that potential offenders will consider the risk of being caught, prosecuted, and sentenced. Laws setting a maximum legal level of blood alcohol content, coupled with mandatory penalties, can have a temporary deterrent effect on a wide population.

  In modern, economically developed societies, crime policies increasingly focus on achieving future reductions in crime. Criminological research plays an increasingly important role in developing future crime policies. Several successful programs directed at high-risk mothers include prenatal health care and parenting classes, which have been shown to reduce delinquent offending when the child becomes an adolescent.

- What are the different types of crimes?

  Crimes are generally classified based on the procedures of the courts. Historically, common law divided crimes into felonies and misdemeanors, but this distinction has become outdated. Modern classifications include arrestable offenses, indictable offenses, and summary offenses. Civil law systems categorize crimes into crime, délit, and contravention, based on severity.

  Examples of crimes include:

  \* Violent crimes Homicide, assault, robbery, and rape.
  \* Property crimes Larceny, burglary, robbery, embezzlement.
  \* White-collar crimes Exploiting social, economic, or technological power for personal or corporate gain, such as bribery, fraud, and cybercrime.
  \* Hate crimes Crimes motivated by hostility toward a victim's group affiliation.
  \* Victimless crimes Activities that do not have a complainant.5 Britannica Sources
- What happens when someone is accused of a crime?

  When someone is accused of a crime, the process begins with an investigation by the police or a public prosecutor to collect evidence. The suspect has the right to gather evidence in their favor and is entitled to legal assistance, which the state often provides if they cannot afford it.

  After the investigation, the accused person appears in court for

  [arraignment](https://www.britannica.com/topic/arraignment/Introduction#ref1), where they are informed of the charges and asked to enter a plea. They may be released on[bail](https://www.britannica.com/topic/bail-law/Introduction#ref1), which is a procedure where a judge sets them at liberty after receiving security to ensure their appearance in court. The amount of bail depends on the severity of the offense and the likelihood of flight.

  The case then proceeds to trial, where a

  [jury](https://www.britannica.com/topic/jury/Size-and-unanimity#ref27065)decides the facts under the supervision of a judge. If found guilty, the penalties depend on whether the crime is a[felony](https://www.britannica.com/topic/felony/Introduction#ref1)(punishable by imprisonment of one year or more) or a misdemeanor (punishable by fines or short jail terms).6 Britannica Sources
- How do courts decide if someone is guilty or innocent?

  In criminal courts, the decision of guilt or innocence involves a complex process. The court must conduct proper proceedings to hear and consider

  [evidence](https://www.britannica.com/topic/evidence-law/Introduction#ref1). The prosecution bears the burden of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

  In common-law systems, the judge ensures legal procedures are followed, and a jury decides guilt or innocence impartially. The jury considers evidence, and the judge instructs them on the law. Evidence can include witnesses, documents, and real evidence. The defense and prosecution present evidence, with the judge ensuring fairness.

  The jury renders a general verdict, and in some cases, special verdicts address specific factual issues. If found guilty, the defendant is sentenced according to law.

## The concept of crime: [criminal codes](https://www.britannica.com/topic/criminal-code)

Criminal behavior is defined by the laws of particular jurisdictions, and there are sometimes vast differences between and even within countries regarding what types of behavior are prohibited. Conduct that is lawful in one country or [jurisdiction](https://www.britannica.com/topic/jurisdiction) may be criminal in another, and activity that amounts to a trivial infraction in one jurisdiction may [constitute](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitute) a serious crime elsewhere. Changing times and social attitudes may lead to changes in criminal law, so that behavior that was once criminal may become lawful. For example, [abortion](https://www.britannica.com/science/abortion-pregnancy), once prohibited except in the most unusual circumstances, is now lawful in many countries, as is homosexual behavior in private between consenting adults in most Western countries, though it remains a serious offense in some parts of the world. Once criminal, [suicide](https://www.britannica.com/topic/physician-assisted-suicide) and attempted suicide have been removed from the scope of criminal law in some jurisdictions. Indeed, in the [U.S. state](https://www.britannica.com/topic/state-United-States-political-subdivision) of [Oregon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Oregon-state) the Death with Dignity Act (passed in 1997) allows terminally ill individuals to end their lives through the use of lethal medications [prescribed by a physician](https://www.britannica.com/topic/assisted-suicide). Nonetheless, the general [trend](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/trend) has been toward increasing the scope of criminal law rather than decreasing it, and it has been more common to find that statutes create new criminal offenses rather than abolishing existing ones. New technologies have given rise to new opportunities for their abuse, which has led to the creation of new legal restrictions. Just as the invention of the motor vehicle led to the development of a whole body of criminal laws designed to regulate its use, so the widening use of computers and especially the [Internet](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Internet) has created the need to legislate against a variety of new abuses and frauds—or old frauds committed in new ways.

In most countries, the criminal law is contained in a single [statute](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/statute), known as the criminal, or penal, code. Although the criminal codes of most English-speaking countries are derived from English criminal law, [England](https://www.britannica.com/place/England) itself has never had a criminal code. English criminal law still consists of a collection of statutes of varying age—the oldest still in force being the Treason Act (1351)—and a set of general principles that are chiefly expressed in the decisions of the courts (case law). England’s lack of a criminal code is not the result of a lack of effort; since the early 19th century, there have been several attempts to create such a code. The first effort (1833–53) was made by two panels of criminal-law commissioners, who systematically surveyed the [prevailing](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/prevailing) state of the criminal law. Confronted by a vast number of often overlapping and inconsistent statutes, the commissioners found that determining precisely what the law provided on any particular topic was enormously difficult. Different statutes covering the same conduct, often with widely varying penalties, allowed for wide judicial discretion and inconsistency in punishments. The commissioners drew up a number of draft codes that were presented to [Parliament](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Parliament), but none was enacted. Eventually, owing to the [judiciary’s](https://www.britannica.com/topic/judiciary) resistance, efforts to codify the criminal law were abandoned, and instead there was a consolidation of most of the criminal law in 1861 into a number of statutes—the Larceny Act, the [Malicious](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Malicious) Damage Act, and the Offences Against the Person Act being among the most important. Because those statutes were consolidations rather than codifications, many of the inconsistencies of the earlier legislation were preserved. The Offences Against the Person Act is still largely in force, though the others have been replaced by more-modern provisions.

Interest in codification was not limited to England. A similar process ensued in India, then under British rule, and a criminal code was written during the 1830s and eventually enacted in 1861. The code remains substantially in force in India, as well as in Pakistan. Certain parts of Africa that were once British colonies also adopted similar codes.

In England, efforts to establish a criminal code resumed in the late 1870s, and in 1879–80 a [draft](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/draft) criminal code bill was again presented to Parliament. Largely the work of the celebrated legal author and [judge](https://www.britannica.com/topic/judge-law) [James Fitzjames Stephen](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Sir-James-Fitzjames-Stephen-1st-Baronet), this code received widespread publicity throughout England and its colonial possessions. Although it was not adopted in England, it was subsequently enacted in Canada (1892) and in several Australian states and British colonies. As interest in codification declined in the 20th century, attempts were made to make specific and particular changes in criminal laws. The permanent Criminal Law Revision Committee, established in 1959, eventually made a variety of specific recommendations, including the elimination of the distinction between felonies and misdemeanors. In addition, the Law Commission, also a permanent body, was established in 1965 with the goal of continually reviewing the entire law, not just the criminal law. In 1981 the commission undertook a new attempt at codification of the criminal law, and a draft code was published in 1989. However, it was severely criticized, and the commission dropped the attempt and instead produced a series of more-specific recommendations.

Criminal-law reform was one of the interests of the [U.S.](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) states in the period following the [American Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/American-Revolution). In the early 1820s, a [comprehensive](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/comprehensive) draft code was prepared for Louisiana, though it was never enacted. Other states also moved to codify their criminal laws. [New York](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-York-state) enacted a criminal code in 1881, setting an example that was eventually followed by most of the states. Because American criminal law is primarily a matter for the individual states (in contrast to Canada, for example, where the national Parliament enacts the criminal code for the whole country), there has been considerable variation in the content of the code from one state to another. In the mid-20th century, reform efforts in the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) led to the publication of the [Model Penal Code](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Model-Penal-Code) (1962), an attempt to rationalize the criminal law by establishing a logical framework for defining offenses and a consistent body of general principles on such matters as criminal intent and the [liability](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/liability) of accomplices. The Model Penal Code had a profound influence on the revision of many individual state codes over the following decades; although never enacted completely, it inspired a long period of criminal-code reform.
