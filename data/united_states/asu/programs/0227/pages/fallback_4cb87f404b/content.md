# Asia

- What is Asia?

  Asia, the largest and most diverse continent, covers the eastern four-fifths of the Eurasian landmass. It is more of a geographic term than a homogeneous continent, with the highest and lowest points on Earth, and the longest coastline.

  The name "Asia" has ancient origins, possibly from the Assyrian word "asu," meaning "east".

  Asia is bounded by the Arctic Ocean to the north, the Pacific Ocean to the east, and the Indian Ocean to the south. The Isthmus of Suez unites Asia with Africa, and the Suez Canal is generally considered the border between them. The land boundary between Asia and Europe is a historical and cultural construct. The total area of Asia is approximately 17,226,200 square miles (44,614,000 square km), about one-third of Earth's land surface.

- How do Asia's people, cultures, and religions differ across the continent?

  Asia is the largest and most diverse continent, home to about three-fifths of the world's population. Its cultural diversity is greater than any other continent, with ethnic types and linguistic systems evolving over long periods in distinct regional homelands.

  ### People and Culture

  The continent's population is unevenly distributed due to climatic factors, with concentrations in western Asia, the Indian subcontinent, and eastern China. Throughout Asia, ethnic groups and languages are richly diverse. Many languages spoken by smaller ethnic groups are becoming functionally extinct due to the influence of politically and economically dominant languages.

  ### Religion

Asia, the world’s largest and most [diverse](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/diverse) [continent](https://www.britannica.com/science/continent). It occupies the eastern four-fifths of the giant Eurasian landmass. Asia is more a geographic term than a [homogeneous](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/homogeneous) continent, and the use of the term to describe such a vast area always carries the potential of obscuring the enormous [diversity](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/diversity) among the regions it [encompasses](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/encompasses). Asia has both the highest and the lowest points on the surface of [Earth](https://www.britannica.com/place/Earth), has the longest coastline of any continent, is subject overall to the world’s widest climatic extremes, and, consequently, produces the most varied forms of vegetation and animal life on Earth. In addition, the peoples of Asia have established the broadest variety of human [adaptation](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/adaptation) found on any of the continents.

The name Asia is ancient, and its origin has been variously explained. The Greeks used it to designate the lands situated to the east of their homeland. It is believed that the name may be derived from the Assyrian word asu, meaning “east.” Another possible explanation is that it was originally a local name given to the plains of [Ephesus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ephesus), which ancient Greeks and Romans extended to refer first to [Anatolia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Anatolia) (contemporary Asia Minor, which is the western extreme of mainland Asia), and then to the known world east of the [Mediterranean Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mediterranean-Sea). When Western explorers reached South and [East Asia](https://www.britannica.com/place/East-Asia) in early modern times, they extended that label to the whole of the [immense](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/immense) landmass.

Asia is bounded by the [Arctic Ocean](https://www.britannica.com/place/Arctic-Ocean) to the north, the [Pacific Ocean](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pacific-Ocean) to the east, the [Indian Ocean](https://www.britannica.com/place/Indian-Ocean) to the south, the [Red Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Red-Sea) (as well as the inland seas of the [Atlantic Ocean](https://www.britannica.com/place/Atlantic-Ocean)—the Mediterranean and the [Black](https://www.britannica.com/place/Black-Sea)) to the southwest, and [Europe](https://www.britannica.com/place/Europe) to the west. Asia is separated from [North America](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-America) to the northeast by the [Bering Strait](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bering-Strait) and from [Australia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Australia) to the southeast by the seas and straits connecting the Indian and Pacific oceans. The Isthmus of Suez unites Asia with [Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Africa), and it is generally agreed that the [Suez Canal](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Suez-Canal) forms the border between them. Two narrow straits, the [Bosporus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bosporus) and the [Dardanelles](https://www.britannica.com/place/Dardanelles), separate Anatolia from the [Balkan Peninsula](https://www.britannica.com/place/Balkans).

The land boundary between Asia and Europe is a historical and cultural construct that has been defined variously; only as a matter of agreement is it tied to a specific borderline. The most convenient geographic boundary—one that has been adopted by most geographers—is a line that runs south from the Arctic Ocean along the [Ural Mountains](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ural-Mountains) and then turns southwest along the Emba River to the northern shore of the [Caspian Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Caspian-Sea); west of the Caspian, the boundary follows the [Kuma-Manych Depression](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kuma-Manych-Depression) to the [Sea of Azov](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sea-of-Azov) and the Kerch Strait of the Black Sea. Thus, the isthmus between the Black and Caspian seas, which culminates in the [Caucasus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Caucasus) mountain range to the south, is part of Asia.

The total area of Asia, including Asian [Russia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Russia) (with the Caucasian isthmus) but excluding the island of [New Guinea](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Guinea), amounts to some 17,226,200 square miles (44,614,000 square km), roughly one-third of the land surface of Earth. The islands—including [Taiwan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Taiwan), those of [Japan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Japan) and [Indonesia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Indonesia), [Sakhalin](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sakhalin-Island) and other islands of Asian Russia, [Sri Lanka](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sri-Lanka), [Cyprus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cyprus), and numerous smaller islands—together [constitute](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitute) 1,240,000 square miles (3,210,000 square km), about 7 percent of the total. (Although New Guinea is mentioned occasionally in this article, it generally is not considered a part of Asia.) The farthest terminal points of the Asian mainland are [Cape Chelyuskin](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cape-Chelyuskin) in north-central [Siberia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Siberia), Russia (77°43′ N), to the north; the tip of the [Malay Peninsula](https://www.britannica.com/place/Malay-Peninsula), Cape Piai, or Bulus (1°16′ N), to the south; Cape Baba in [Turkey](https://www.britannica.com/place/Turkey) (26°4′ E) to the west; and [Cape Dezhnev (Dezhnyov)](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cape-Dezhnyov), or East Cape (169°40′ W), in northeastern Siberia, overlooking the Bering Strait, to the east.

Asia has the highest average elevation of the continents and contains the greatest relative relief. The tallest peak in the world, [Mount Everest](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mount-Everest), which reaches an elevation of 29,035 feet (8,850 metres; see [Researcher’s Note: Height of Mount Everest](/place/Asia/additional-info#Researchers-Note)); the lowest place on Earth’s land surface, the [Dead Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Dead-Sea), measured in the mid-2010s at about 1,410 feet (430 metres) below [sea level](https://www.britannica.com/science/sea-level); and the world’s deepest continental trough, occupied by [Lake Baikal](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lake-Baikal), which is [5,315 feet (1,620 metres)](/place/Asia/additional-info#Researchers-Note) deep and whose bottom lies 3,822 feet (1,165 metres) below sea level, are all located in Asia. Those physiographic extremes and the overall predominance of mountain belts and plateaus are the result of the collision of tectonic plates. In geologic terms, Asia [comprises](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/comprises) several very ancient continental platforms and other blocks of land that merged over the eons. Most of those units had coalesced as a continental landmass by about 160 million years ago, when the core of the [Indian subcontinent](https://www.britannica.com/place/Indian-subcontinent) broke off from Africa and began drifting northeastward to collide with the southern flank of Asia about 50 million to 40 million years ago. The northeastward movement of the subcontinent continues at about 2.4 inches (6 cm) per year. The impact and pressure continue to raise the [Plateau of Tibet](https://www.britannica.com/place/Plateau-of-Tibet) and the [Himalayas](https://www.britannica.com/place/Himalayas).

Asia’s coastline—some 39,000 miles (62,800 km) in length—is, variously, high and mountainous, low and alluvial, terraced as a result of the land’s having been [uplifted](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/uplifted), or “drowned” where the land has subsided. The specific features of the coastline in some areas—especially in the east and southeast—are the result of active volcanism; thermal abrasion of permafrost (caused by a combination of the action of breaking waves and thawing), as in northeastern Siberia; and coral growth, as in the areas to the south and southeast. Accreting sandy beaches also occur in many areas, such as along the [Bay of Bengal](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bay-of-Bengal) and the [Gulf of Thailand](https://www.britannica.com/place/Gulf-of-Thailand).

The mountain systems of [Central Asia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Central-Asia) not only have provided the continent’s great rivers with water from their melting snows but also have formed a forbidding natural barrier that has influenced the movement of peoples in the area. Migration across those barriers has been possible only through mountain passes. A historical movement of population from the arid zones of Central Asia has followed the mountain passes into the Indian subcontinent. More recent migrations have originated in [China](https://www.britannica.com/place/China), with destinations throughout [Southeast Asia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Southeast-Asia). The Korean and Japanese peoples and, to a lesser extent, the Chinese have remained ethnically more [homogeneous](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/homogeneous) than the populations of other Asian countries.

Asia’s population is unevenly distributed, mainly because of climatic factors. There is a concentration of population in western Asia as well as great concentrations in the Indian subcontinent and the eastern half of China. There are also appreciable concentrations in the Pacific borderlands and on the islands, but vast areas of Central and North Asia—whose forbidding climates limit agricultural productivity—have remained sparsely populated. Nonetheless, Asia, the most populous of the continents, contains some three-fifths of the world’s people.

Asia is the birthplace of all the world’s major religions—[Buddhism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Buddhism), [Christianity](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Christianity), [Hinduism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hinduism), [Islam](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Islam), and [Judaism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Judaism)—and of many minor ones. Of those, only Christianity developed primarily outside of Asia; it [exerts](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/exerts) little influence on the continent, though many Asian countries have Christian minorities. Buddhism has had a greater impact outside its birthplace in [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/India) and is prevalent in various forms in China, [South Korea](https://www.britannica.com/place/South-Korea), Japan, the Southeast Asian countries, and Sri Lanka. Islam has spread out of [Arabia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Arabia-peninsula-Asia) eastward to South and Southeast Asia. Hinduism has been mostly confined to the Indian subcontinent.
