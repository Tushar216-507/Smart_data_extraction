equine, one of the [mammal](https://www.britannica.com/animal/mammal) family of Equidae (order Perissodactyla) that includes the modern [horses](https://www.britannica.com/animal/horse), [zebras](https://www.britannica.com/animal/zebra), and [asses](https://www.britannica.com/animal/ass), as well as more than 60 [species](https://www.britannica.com/science/species-taxon) known only from [fossils](https://www.britannica.com/science/fossil).

All six modern members of the family are placed in the [genus](https://www.britannica.com/science/genus-taxon) [Equus](https://www.britannica.com/animal/Equus-mammal-genus). Only the races of E. caballus (including the [myriad](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/myriad) domestic strains) are called [horses;](https://www.britannica.com/animal/horse) three species ([E. zebra](https://www.britannica.com/animal/mountain-zebra), [E. burchelli](https://www.britannica.com/animal/plains-zebra), and [E. grevyi](https://www.britannica.com/animal/Grevys-zebra)) are called zebras; and two ([E. asinus](https://www.britannica.com/animal/donkey) and [E. hemionus](https://www.britannica.com/animal/Asiatic-wild-ass)) are usually called wild asses.

[Wild horses](https://www.britannica.com/animal/wild-horse) once inhabited much of northern [Eurasia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Eurasia), primarily in open areas. They were rather small, short-legged animals, compared with their domesticated descendants, standing only about 120 to 130 cm (47 to 51 inches) at the shoulder. In the two millennia bc, horses from many wild populations were domesticated; often the remainder of the wild individuals were exterminated. By the early 19th century, two races were still extant: the [tarpan](https://www.britannica.com/animal/tarpan) (E. caballus caballus), found in eastern Europe until the middle of the century, and [Przewalski’s horse](https://www.britannica.com/animal/Przewalskis-horse) (E. caballus przewalskii, often considered a distinct species, E. przewalskii), which [inhabited](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/inhabited) the remote [Steppe](https://www.britannica.com/place/the-Steppe) region between China and Mongolia.
