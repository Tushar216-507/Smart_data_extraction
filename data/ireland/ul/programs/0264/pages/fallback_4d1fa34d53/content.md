Mathematics is a field of [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge) concerned with abstract concepts such as [numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number), [geometric shapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape), [sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_(mathematics)), [functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_(mathematics)), and [probabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability). It uses logical reasoning and proof to study and establish their properties, often expressed as [theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorem), [formulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formula), and [equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equation). Mathematics is used to model and solve problems in [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), [technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), and everyday life.

There are many areas of mathematics, including [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory) (the study of integers and their properties), [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra) (the study of operations and the structures they form), [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry) (the study of shapes and spaces that contain them), [analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_analysis) (the quantitative study of approximation and convergence), and [set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_theory) (presently used as a foundation for all mathematics).

Mathematics involves the description and manipulation of [abstract objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_object) that are either [abstractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstraction_(mathematics)) from nature or purely abstract entities that are stipulated to have certain properties, called [axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom). Mathematics uses pure [reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reason) to prove the properties of objects through [proofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_proof), which consist of a succession of applications of [deductive rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inference_rule) to already established results. These results, called [theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorem), include previously proved theorems, axioms, and—in case of abstraction from nature—some basic properties that are considered true starting points of the theory under consideration.

Mathematics is essential in the [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine), [finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance), [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), and the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_sciences). Although mathematics is widely used to model empirical phenomena, its results are established by deductive proof rather than by experiment. The relationship between mathematical truth, logic, and reality is a [subject of philosophical debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_mathematics). Some areas of mathematics, such as [game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory), are developed in close correlation with their applications and are often grouped under [applied mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_mathematics). Other areas are developed independently from any application but often find practical applications later.

Mathematical written records first appeared in [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_mathematics) and [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_mathematics), but the concept of proof and its associated [mathematical rigor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_rigor) began in [Ancient Greek mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics), exemplified in [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid)'s [Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_Elements). [Mathematics was primarily divided into geometry and arithmetic until the 16th and 17th centuries, when algebra](#cite_note-6) [and](#cite_note-7) [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus) evolved into new fields. Since then, the interaction between mathematical innovations and [scientific discoveries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_scientific_discoveries) has led to a correlated increase in the development of both. [At the end of the 19th century, the](#cite_note-8) [foundational crisis of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundational_crisis_of_mathematics) led to the systematic use of the [axiomatic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_method), [which heralded a dramatic increase in the number of mathematical areas and their fields of application. The contemporary](#cite_note-Kleiner_1991-9) [Mathematics Subject Classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_Subject_Classification) lists more than sixty first-level areas of mathematics.

## Areas of mathematics

Before the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance), mathematics was divided into two main areas: [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic), regarding the study and manipulation of numbers, and [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry), regarding the study of shapes. [Some types of](#cite_note-12) [pseudoscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscience), such as [numerology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerology) and [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology), were not then clearly distinguished from mathematics.

Beginning with the Renaissance, two more areas became predominant. New [mathematical notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_notation) led to [modern algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra) which, roughly speaking, begins with the study and manipulation of [algebraic expressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_expression). [Calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus), consisting of the two subfields [differential calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_calculus) and [integral calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_calculus), originated with geometry but evolved into the study of [continuous functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_functions), which model the typically [nonlinear relationships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_system) between varying quantities, as represented by [variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_(mathematics)). This division into four main areas—arithmetic, geometry, algebra, and calculus[—endured until the end of the 19th century. Other areas that were previously studied by mathematicians, such as](#cite_note-14) [celestial mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_mechanics) and [solid mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_mechanics), are now considered as belonging to physics. [The subject of](#cite_note-15) [combinatorics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorics) has been studied for much of recorded history, yet did not become a separate branch of mathematics until the 17th century.

At the end of the 19th century, the [foundational crisis in mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundational_crisis_in_mathematics) and the systematic use of the [axiomatic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_method) led to an explosion of new areas of mathematics. [The 2020](#cite_note-Kleiner_1991-9) [Mathematics Subject Classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_Subject_Classification) contains no less than sixty-three first-level areas. [Some of these areas correspond to the older division, as is true regarding](#cite_note-MSC2020-11) [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory) (the modern name for [higher arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_arithmetic)) and geometry. Several other first-level areas have "geometry" in their names or are otherwise commonly considered part of geometry. Algebra and calculus do not appear as first-level areas but are split into several first-level areas. Other first-level areas emerged during the 20th century or had not previously been considered as mathematics, such as [mathematical logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_logic) and [foundations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundations_of_mathematics).

### Number theory

Number theory evolved from the manipulation of [numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number), that is, [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_number) and later expanded to [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer) and [rational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_number) Number theory was once called arithmetic, but nowadays this term is mostly used for [numerical calculations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_calculation). [The study of numbers arguably dates back to ancient](#cite_note-18) [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_mathematics) and probably [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_China), but developed into a distinct discipline in [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_mathematics). Two prominent early number theorists were [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid) and [Diophantus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantus) of [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria). [The modern study of number theory in its abstract form is largely attributed to](#cite_note-19) [Pierre de Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Fermat) and [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler). The field came to full fruition with the contributions of [Adrien-Marie Legendre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrien-Marie_Legendre) and [Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss).

Many easily stated number problems have solutions that require sophisticated methods, often from across mathematics. A prominent example is [Fermat's Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_Last_Theorem). This conjecture was stated in 1637 by Pierre de Fermat, but it [was proved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiles's_proof_of_Fermat's_Last_Theorem) only in 1994 by [Andrew Wiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Wiles), who used tools including [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory) from [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry), [category theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_theory), and [homological algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homological_algebra). [Another example is](#cite_note-21) [Goldbach's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldbach's_conjecture), which asserts that every even integer greater than 2 is the sum of two [prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number). Stated in 1742 by [Christian Goldbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Goldbach), it remains unproven despite considerable effort.

Number theory includes several subareas, including [analytic number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_number_theory), [algebraic number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_number_theory), [geometry of numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry_of_numbers) (method oriented), [Diophantine analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_analysis), and [transcendence theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_theory) (problem oriented).

### Geometry

Geometry is one of the oldest branches of mathematics. It started with empirical recipes concerning shapes, such as [lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_(geometry)), [angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle) and [circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle), which were developed mainly for the needs of [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying) and [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture), but has since blossomed out into many other subfields.

A fundamental innovation was the ancient Greeks' introduction of the concept of [proofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_proof), which require that every assertion must be proved. For example, it is not sufficient to verify by [measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement) that, say, two lengths are equal; their equality must be proven via reasoning from previously accepted results ([theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorem)) and a few basic statements. The basic statements are not subject to proof because they are self-evident ([postulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postulate)), or are part of the definition of the subject of study ([axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom)). This principle, foundational for all mathematics, was first elaborated for geometry, and was systematized by [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid) around 300 BC in his book [Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_Elements).

The resulting [Euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_geometry) is the study of shapes and their arrangements [constructed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straightedge_and_compass_construction) from lines, [planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_(geometry)) and circles in the [Euclidean plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane) ([plane geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_geometry)) and the three-dimensional [Euclidean space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_space).

Euclidean geometry was developed without change of methods or scope until the 17th century, when [René Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Descartes) introduced what is now called [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates). This constituted a major [change of paradigm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradigm_shift): Instead of defining [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number) as lengths of [line segments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_segments) (see [number line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_line)), it allowed the representation of points using their coordinates, which are numbers. Algebra (and later, calculus) can thus be used to solve geometrical problems. Geometry was split into two new subfields: [synthetic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_geometry), which uses purely geometrical methods, and [analytic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_geometry), which uses coordinates systemically.

Analytic geometry allows the study of [curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curve) unrelated to circles and lines. Such curves can be defined as the [graph of functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_of_a_function), the study of which led to [differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry). They can also be defined as [implicit equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_equation), often [polynomial equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_equation) (which spawned [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry)). Analytic geometry also makes it possible to consider Euclidean spaces of higher than three dimensions.

In the 19th century, mathematicians discovered [non-Euclidean geometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometries), which do not follow the [parallel postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_postulate). By questioning that postulate's truth, this discovery has been viewed as joining [Russell's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell's_paradox) in revealing the [foundational crisis of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundational_crisis_of_mathematics). This aspect of the crisis was solved by systematizing the axiomatic method, and adopting that the truth of the chosen axioms is not a mathematical problem. [In turn, the axiomatic method allows for the study of various geometries obtained either by changing the axioms or by considering properties that](#cite_note-Kleiner_1991-9) [do not change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_(mathematics)) under specific transformations of the [space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_(mathematics)).

Today's subareas of geometry include:

- [Projective geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_geometry), introduced in the 16th century by[Girard Desargues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girard_Desargues), extends Euclidean geometry by adding[points at infinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Points_at_infinity)at which[parallel lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_lines)intersect. This simplifies many aspects of classical geometry by unifying the treatments for intersecting and parallel lines.
- [Affine geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_geometry), the study of properties relative to[parallelism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_(geometry))and independent from the concept of length.
- [Differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry), the study of curves, surfaces, and their generalizations, which are defined using[differentiable functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable_function).
- [Manifold theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_theory), the study of shapes that are not necessarily embedded in a larger space.
- [Riemannian geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_geometry), the study of distance properties in curved spaces.
- [Algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry), the study of curves, surfaces, and their generalizations, which are defined using[polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial).
- [Topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology), the study of properties that are kept under[continuous deformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_deformation).
  - [Algebraic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_topology), the use in topology of algebraic methods, mainly[homological algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homological_algebra).
- [Discrete geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_geometry), the study of finite configurations in geometry.
- [Convex geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_geometry), the study of[convex sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_set), which takes its importance from its applications in[optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_optimization).
- [Complex geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_geometry), the geometry obtained by replacing real numbers with[complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number).

### Algebra

Algebra is the art of manipulating [equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equation) and formulas. [Diophantus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantus) (3rd century) and [al-Khwarizmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khwarizmi) (9th century) were the two main precursors of algebra. [Diophantus solved some equations involving unknown natural numbers by deducing new relations until he obtained the solution.](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKleiner2007"History_of_Classical_Algebra"_pp._3–5-32) [Al-Khwarizmi introduced systematic methods for transforming equations, such as moving a term from one side of an equation into the other side.](#cite_note-33) [The term algebra is derived from the](#cite_note-34) [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) word al-jabr meaning 'the reunion of broken parts' that he used for naming one of these methods in the title of [his main treatise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Compendious_Book_on_Calculation_by_Completion_and_Balancing).

Algebra became an area in its own right only with [François Viète](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Viète) (1540–1603), who introduced the use of variables for representing unknown or unspecified numbers. [Variables allow mathematicians to describe the operations that have to be done on the numbers represented using](#cite_note-37) [mathematical formulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_formulas).

Until the 19th century, algebra consisted mainly of the study of [linear equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_equation) (presently [linear algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_algebra)), and polynomial equations in a single [unknown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unknown_(algebra)), which were called algebraic equations (a term still in use, although it may be ambiguous). During the 19th century, mathematicians began to use variables to represent things other than numbers (such as [matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_(mathematics)), [modular integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_arithmetic), and [geometric transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_transformation)), on which generalizations of arithmetic operations are often valid. [The concept of](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKleiner2007"History_of_Linear_Algebra"_pp._79–101-39) [algebraic structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_structure) addresses this, consisting of a [set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_(mathematics)) whose elements are unspecified, of operations acting on the elements of the set, and rules that these operations must follow. The scope of algebra thus grew to include the study of algebraic structures. This object of algebra was called modern algebra or [abstract algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_algebra), as established by the influence and works of [Emmy Noether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmy_Noether),[and popularized by](#cite_note-40) [Van der Waerden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_der_Waerden)'s book [Moderne Algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moderne_Algebra).

Some types of algebraic structures have useful and often fundamental properties, in many areas of mathematics. Their study became autonomous parts of algebra, and include:

- [group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory)
- [field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(mathematics))
- [vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_space), whose study is essentially the same as[linear algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_algebra)
- [ring theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_theory)
- [commutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra), which is the study of[commutative rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_ring), includes the study of[polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial), and is a foundational part of[algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry)
- [homological algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homological_algebra)
- [Lie algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebra)and[Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_group)theory
- [Boolean algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_algebra), which is widely used for the study of the logical structure of[computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer)

The study of types of algebraic structures as [mathematical objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_object) is the purpose of [universal algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_algebra) and [category theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_theory). [The latter applies to every](#cite_note-41) [mathematical structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_structure) (not only algebraic ones). At its origin, it was introduced, together with homological algebra for allowing the algebraic study of non-algebraic objects such as [topological spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_space); this particular area of application is called [algebraic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_topology).

### Calculus and analysis

Calculus, formerly called infinitesimal calculus, was introduced independently and simultaneously by 17th-century mathematicians [Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton) and [Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz). [It is fundamentally the study of the relationship between variables that depend continuously on each other. Calculus was expanded in the 18th century by](#cite_note-43) [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler) with the introduction of the concept of a [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_(mathematics)) and many other results. [Presently, "calculus" refers mainly to the elementary part of this theory, and "analysis" is commonly used for advanced parts.](#cite_note-44)

Analysis is further subdivided into [real analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_analysis), where variables represent [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number), and [complex analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analysis), where variables represent [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number). Analysis includes many subareas shared by other areas of mathematics which include:

- [Multivariable calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariable_calculus)
- [Functional analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_analysis), where variables represent varying functions
- [Integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integration_(mathematics)),[measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory)and[potential theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_theory), all strongly related with[probability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory)on a[continuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_(set_theory))
- [Ordinary differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_differential_equation)
- [Partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equation)
- [Numerical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_analysis), mainly devoted to the computation of solutions of ordinary and partial differential equations that arise in many applications

### Discrete mathematics

Discrete mathematics, broadly speaking, is the study of individual, [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) mathematical objects. An example is the set of all integers. [Because the objects of study here are discrete, the methods of calculus and mathematical analysis do not directly apply.](#cite_note-46)[Algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithm)—especially their [implementation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implementation) and [computational complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_complexity)—play a major role in discrete mathematics.

The [four color theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_color_theorem) and [optimal sphere packing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler_conjecture) were two major problems of discrete mathematics solved in the second half of the 20th century. [The](#cite_note-49) [P versus NP problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_versus_NP_problem), which remains open to this day, is also important for discrete mathematics, since its solution would potentially impact a large number of [computationally difficult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computationally_expensive) problems.

Discrete mathematics includes:

- [Combinatorics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorics), the art of enumerating mathematical objects that satisfy some given constraints. Originally, these objects were elements or[subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subset)of a given[set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_(mathematics)); this has been extended to various objects, which establishes a strong link between combinatorics and other parts of discrete mathematics. For example, discrete geometry includes counting configurations of[geometric shapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_shape).
- [Graph theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_theory)and[hypergraphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergraph)
- [Coding theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coding_theory), including[error correcting codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_correcting_code)and a part of[cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptography)
- [Matroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matroid)theory
- [Discrete geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_geometry)
- [Discrete probability distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_probability_distribution)
- [Game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory)(although[continuous games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_game)are also studied, most common games, such as[chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess)and[poker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poker)are discrete)
- [Discrete optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_optimization), including[combinatorial optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_optimization),[integer programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_programming),[constraint programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constraint_programming)

### Mathematical logic and set theory

The two subjects of mathematical logic and set theory have belonged to mathematics since the end of the 19th century. [Before this period, sets were not considered to be mathematical objects, and](#cite_note-52) [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic), although used for mathematical proofs, belonged to [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) and was not specifically studied by mathematicians.

Before [Cantor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Cantor)'s study of [infinite sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_set), mathematicians were reluctant to consider [actually infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actually_infinite) collections, and considered [infinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinity) to be the result of endless [enumeration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enumeration). Cantor's work offended many mathematicians not only by considering actually infinite sets [but by showing that this implies different sizes of infinity, per](#cite_note-54) [Cantor's diagonal argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor's_diagonal_argument). This led to the [controversy over Cantor's set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controversy_over_Cantor's_theory). [In the same period, various areas of mathematics concluded the former intuitive definitions of the basic mathematical objects were insufficient for ensuring](#cite_note-55) [mathematical rigor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_rigor).

This became the foundational crisis of mathematics. [It was eventually solved in mainstream mathematics by systematizing the axiomatic method inside a](#cite_note-57) [formalized set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo–Fraenkel_set_theory). Roughly speaking, each mathematical object is defined by the set of all similar objects and the properties that these objects must have. [For example, in](#cite_note-Warner_2013-17) [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic), the natural numbers are defined by "zero is a number", "each number has a unique successor", "each number but zero has a unique predecessor", and some rules of reasoning. [This](#cite_note-58) [mathematical abstraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_abstraction) from reality is embodied in the modern philosophy of [formalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formalism_(philosophy_of_mathematics)), as founded by [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert) around 1910.

The "nature" of the objects defined this way is a philosophical problem that mathematicians leave to philosophers, even if many mathematicians have opinions on this nature, and use their opinion—sometimes called "intuition"—to guide their study and proofs. The approach allows considering "logics" (that is, sets of allowed deducing rules), theorems, proofs, etc. as mathematical objects, and to prove theorems about them. For example, [Gödel's incompleteness theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel's_incompleteness_theorems) assert, roughly speaking that, in every [consistent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consistent) [formal system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_system) that contains the natural numbers, there are theorems that are true (that is provable in a stronger system), but not provable inside the system. [This approach to the foundations of mathematics was challenged during the first half of the 20th century by mathematicians led by](#cite_note-Raatikainen_2005-60) [Brouwer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._E._J._Brouwer), who promoted [intuitionistic logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuitionistic_logic) (which explicitly lacks the [law of excluded middle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_excluded_middle)).

These problems and debates led to a wide expansion of mathematical logic, with subareas such as [model theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_theory) (modeling some logical theories inside other theories), [proof theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_theory), [type theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_theory), [computability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computability_theory) and [computational complexity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_complexity_theory). [Although these aspects of mathematical logic were introduced before the rise of](#cite_note-MSC-10) [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer), their use in [compiler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compiler) design, [formal verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_verification), [program analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Program_analysis), [proof assistants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_assistant) and other aspects of [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), contributed in turn to the expansion of these logical theories.

### Computational mathematics

Computational mathematics is the study of [mathematical problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_problem) that are typically too large for human computing capacity.

Part of computational mathematics involves [numerical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_analysis), which is devoted to computation with approximations of [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) ([floating-point arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating-point_arithmetic)). Numerical analysis provides methods for problems in [analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_(mathematics)) using [functional analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_analysis) and [approximation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximation_theory). Numerical analysis broadly includes the study of [approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximation) and [discretization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discretization), with special focus on [rounding errors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rounding_error). [Numerical analysis and, more broadly, scientific computing, also study non-analytic topics of mathematical science, especially algorithmic-](#cite_note-66)[matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_linear_algebra)-and-[graph theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_theory).

Another area of computational mathematics is [computer algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_algebra), which consists mainly of the manipulation of mathematical formulas on a computer.

[Automated theorem proving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automated_theorem_proving), which include [proof assistants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_assistant) and [proof checkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_checker) is another area of computational mathematics that is used for [formal verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_verification) and allowed [formal proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_proof) of difficult theorems of mathematics such [Feit–Thompson theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feit–Thompson_theorem) on [finite groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_groups).

[Algorithmics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmics), a large part of [discrete mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_mathematics), and [public key cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_key_cryptography) are also often considered as a part of computational mathematics.

## History

### Etymology

The word mathematics comes from the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) word máthēma ([μάθημα](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/μάθημα#English)), meaning 'something learned, knowledge, mathematics', and the derived expression mathēmatikḗ tékhnē (μαθηματικὴ τέχνη), meaning 'mathematical science'. It entered the English language during the [Late Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Middle_English) period through French and Latin.

Traditionally, one of the two main schools of thought in [Pythagoreanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoreanism) was known as the mathēmatikoi (μαθηματικοί)—which at the time meant "learners" rather than "mathematicians" in the modern sense. The Pythagoreans were likely the first to constrain the use of the word to just the study of [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic) and geometry. By the time of [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle) (384–322BC) this meaning was fully established.

In Latin and English, until around 1700, the term mathematics more commonly meant "[astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology)" (or sometimes "[astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy)") rather than "mathematics"; the meaning gradually changed to its present one from about 1500 to 1800. This change has resulted in several mistranslations: For example, [Saint Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Augustine)'s warning that Christians should beware of mathematici, meaning "astrologers", is sometimes mistranslated as a condemnation of mathematicians.

The apparent [plural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plural) form in English goes back to the Latin [neuter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuter_(grammar)) plural mathematica ([Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero)), based on the Greek plural ta mathēmatiká (τὰ μαθηματικά) and means roughly "all things mathematical", although it is plausible that English borrowed only the adjective mathematic(al) and formed the noun mathematics anew, after the pattern of [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics) and [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics), inherited from Greek. [In English, the noun mathematics takes a singular verb. It is often shortened to maths](#cite_note-70) [or, in North America, math.](#cite_note-71)

### Ancient

In addition to recognizing how to [count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counting) physical objects, [prehistoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric) peoples may have also known how to count abstract quantities, like time—days, seasons, or years.

Archaeological evidence has suggested that the Ancient Egyptian counting system had origins in Sub-Saharan Africa. [Also, fractal geometry designs which are widespread among Sub-Saharan African cultures are also found in Egyptian architecture and cosmological signs.](#cite_note-75)[The](#cite_note-76) [Ishango bone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishango_bone), according to scholar [Alexander Marshack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Marshack), may have influenced the later development of mathematics in Egypt as, like some entries on the Ishango bone, Egyptian arithmetic also made use of multiplication by 2; this however, is disputed. [Megalithic structures located in](#cite_note-77) [Nabta Playa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabta_Playa), Upper Egypt featured [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), calendar arrangements in alignment with the heliacal rising of [Sirius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirius) and supported calibration of the yearly calendar for the annual Nile flood. [Ancient](#cite_note-78) [Nubians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubians) established a system of geometric rules which served as the basis for initial [sunclocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundials). Nubians also exercised a [trigonometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry) methodology comparable to their Egyptian counterparts.

Evidence for more complex mathematics does not appear until around 3000BC, when the [Babylonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia) and Egyptians began using arithmetic, algebra, and geometry for taxation and other financial calculations, for building and construction, and for astronomy. [The oldest mathematical texts from](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKline1990Chapter_1-83) [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia) and [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) are from 2000 to 1800BC. [Many early texts mention](#cite_note-84) [Pythagorean triples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_triple) and so, by inference, the [Pythagorean theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem) seems to be the most ancient and widespread mathematical concept after basic arithmetic and geometry. It is in Babylonian mathematics that [elementary arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_arithmetic) ([addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Addition), [subtraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtraction), [multiplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication), and [division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_(mathematics))) first appear in the archaeological record. The Babylonians also possessed a place-value system and used a [sexagesimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexagesimal) numeral system which is still in use today for measuring angles and time.

By the 5th century BC, [Greek mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) began to emerge as a distinct discipline and some [Ancient Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greeks) such as the [Pythagoreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoreans) appeared to have considered it a subject in its own right. [Around 300 BC,](#cite_note-86) [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid) organized mathematical knowledge by way of postulates and first principles, which evolved into the axiomatic method that is used in mathematics today, consisting of definition, axiom, theorem, and proof. [His book,](#cite_note-87) [Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_Elements), covers geometry and number theory and is widely considered the most successful and influential textbook of all time. [Another notable mathematician of antiquity is](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyer1991"Euclid_of_Alexandria"_p._119-88) [Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes) of [Syracuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syracuse,_Italy) (c.287– c.212 BC). [He developed methods for calculating the surface area and volume of](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyer1991"Archimedes_of_Syracuse"_p._120-89) [solids of revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solids_of_revolution), including using the [method of exhaustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_exhaustion) to calculate the [area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area) under the arc of a [parabola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabola) with the [summation of an infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Series_(mathematics)), in a manner reminiscent of modern calculus. [Other notable achievements of Greek mathematics are](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyer1991"Archimedes_of_Syracuse"_p._130-90) [conic sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_sections) ([Apollonius of Perga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollonius_of_Perga), 3rd century BC),[trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry) ([Hipparchus of Nicaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hipparchus_of_Nicaea), 2nd century BC), [and the beginnings of pre-modern algebra (Diophantus, 3rd century AD).](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyer1991"Greek_Trigonometry_and_Mensuration"_p._162-92)

The [Hindu–Arabic numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu–Arabic_numeral_system) and the rules for the use of its operations, in use throughout the world today, evolved over the course of the first millennium AD in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_mathematics) and were transmitted to the [Western world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world) via [Islamic mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_mathematics). [Other notable developments of Indian mathematics include the modern definition and approximation of](#cite_note-94) [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) and [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine), and an early form of [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series).

### Medieval and later

During the [Golden Age of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Age_of_Islam), especially during the 9th and 10thcenturies, mathematics saw many important innovations building on Greek mathematics. The most notable achievement of Islamic mathematics was the development of [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra). Other achievements of the Islamic period include advances in [spherical trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_trigonometry) and the addition of the [decimal point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_point) to the Arabic numeral system. [Many notable mathematicians from this period were Persian, such as](#cite_note-97) [Al-Khwarizmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khwarizmi), [Omar Khayyam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omar_Khayyam) and [Sharaf al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharaf_al-Dīn_al-Ṭūsī). [The Greek and Arabic mathematical texts were in turn translated to Latin during the Middle Ages and made available in Europe.](#cite_note-98)

During the [early modern period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period), mathematics began to develop at an accelerating pace in [Western Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Europe), with innovations that revolutionized mathematics, such as the introduction of variables and [symbolic notation](#Symbolic_notation_and_terminology) attributed to [François Viète](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Viète) (1540–1603), [the introduction of](#cite_note-100) [logarithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_logarithms) by [John Napier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Napier) in 1614, [which greatly simplified numerical calculations, especially for](#cite_note-101) [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) and [marine navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_navigation), the introduction of Cartesian coordinates by [René Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Descartes) (1596–1650) for reducing geometry to algebra, [and the development of calculus by](#cite_note-102) [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton) (1643–1727) and [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz) (1646–1716).[Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler) (1707–1783) unified and built upon these innovations with a standardized terminology, and complemented them with the discovery and proofs of numerous theorems.

Perhaps the foremost mathematician of the 19th century was the German mathematician [Carl Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gauss), who made numerous contributions to fields such as algebra, analysis, [differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry), [matrix theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_theory), number theory, and [statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics). [In the early 20th century,](#cite_note-105) [Kurt Gödel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Gödel) transformed mathematics by publishing [his incompleteness theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel's_incompleteness_theorems), which show in part that any consistent axiomatic system—if powerful enough to describe arithmetic—will contain true propositions that cannot be proved.

Mathematics has since been greatly extended, and there has been a fruitful interaction between mathematics and [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science), to the benefit of both. Mathematical discoveries continue to be made to this very day. According to Mikhail B. Sevryuk, in the January2006 issue of the [Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulletin_of_the_American_Mathematical_Society), "The number of papers and books included in the [Mathematical Reviews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Reviews) (MR) database since 1940 (the first year of operation of MR) is now more than 1.9million, and more than 75thousand items are added to the database each year. The overwhelming majority of works in this ocean contain new mathematical theorems and their proofs."

## Symbolic notation and terminology

Mathematical notation is widely used in science and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) for representing complex [concepts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept) and [properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_(philosophy)) in a concise, unambiguous, and accurate way. This notation consists of [symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_mathematical_symbols) used for representing [operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_(mathematics)), unspecified numbers, [relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_(mathematics)) and any other mathematical objects, and then assembling them into [expressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expression_(mathematics)) and formulas. [More precisely, numbers and other mathematical objects are represented by symbols called variables, which are generally](#cite_note-107) [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_alphabet) or [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet) letters, and often include [subscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subscript). Operation and relations are generally represented by specific symbols or [glyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyph), [such as + (](#cite_note-108)[plus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plus_sign)), × ([multiplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication_sign)), ([integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_sign)), = ([equal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equals_sign)), and < ([less than](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Less-than_sign)). [All these symbols are generally grouped according to specific rules to form expressions and formulas.](#cite_note-AMS-109) [Normally, expressions and formulas do not appear alone, but are included in sentences of the current language, where expressions play the role of](#cite_note-110) [noun phrases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noun_phrase) and formulas play the role of [clauses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clause).

Mathematics has developed a rich terminology covering a broad range of fields that study the properties of various abstract, idealized objects and how they interact. It is based on rigorous [definitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_definition) that provide a standard foundation for communication. An axiom or [postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postulate) is a mathematical statement that is taken to be true without need of proof. If a mathematical statement has yet to be proven (or disproven), it is termed a [conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjecture). Through a series of rigorous arguments employing [deductive reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_reasoning), a statement that is [proven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_proof) to be true becomes a theorem. A specialized theorem that is mainly used to prove another theorem is called a [lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemma_(mathematics)). A proven instance that forms part of a more general finding is termed a [corollary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corollary).

Numerous technical terms used in mathematics are [neologisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neologism), such as [polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial) and [homeomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphism). [Other technical terms are words of the common language that are used in an accurate meaning that may differ slightly from their common meaning. For example, in mathematics, "](#cite_note-112)[or](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_disjunction)" means "one, the other or both", while, in common language, it is either ambiguous or means "one or the other but not both" (in mathematics, the latter is called "[exclusive or](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_or)"). Finally, many mathematical terms are common words that are used with a completely different meaning. [This may lead to sentences that are correct and true mathematical assertions, but appear to be nonsense to people who do not have the required background. For example, "every](#cite_note-113) [free module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_module) is [flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_module)" and "a [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(mathematics)) is always a [ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_(mathematics))".

## Relationship with sciences

Mathematics is used in most [sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) for [modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_model) phenomena, which then allows predictions to be made from experimental laws. [The independence of mathematical truth from any experimentation implies that the accuracy of such predictions depends only on the adequacy of the model.](#cite_note-114) [Inaccurate predictions, rather than being caused by invalid mathematical concepts, imply the need to change the mathematical model used.](#cite_note-115) [For example, the](#cite_note-116) [perihelion precession of Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perihelion_precession_of_Mercury) could only be explained after the emergence of [Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein)'s [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity), which replaced [Newton's law of gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_gravitation) as a better mathematical model.

There is still a [philosophical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_mathematics) debate whether mathematics is a science. However, in practice, mathematicians are typically grouped with scientists, and mathematics shares much in common with the physical sciences. Like them, it is [falsifiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falsifiable), which means in mathematics that, if a result or a theory is wrong, this can be proved by providing a [counterexample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterexample). Similarly as in science, [theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_theory) and results (theorems) are often obtained from [experimentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimentation). [In mathematics, the experimentation may consist of computation on selected examples or of the study of figures or other representations of mathematical objects (often mind representations without physical support). For example, when asked how he came about his theorems, Gauss once replied "durch planmässiges Tattonieren" (through systematic experimentation).](#cite_note-118) [However, some authors emphasize that mathematics differs from the modern notion of science by not relying on empirical evidence.](#cite_note-119)

### Pure and applied mathematics

Until the 19th century, there was no clear distinction between pure and applied mathematics as understood today. [The distinction between developing mathematics for its own sake or for its applications was rather fluid: natural numbers and arithmetic were introduced for the need of counting, and geometry was motivated by surveying, architecture, and astronomy, but both subjects quickly stood on their own. Later,](#cite_note-Ferreirós_2007-124) [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton) used infinitesimal calculus in part to help explain the movement of the [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet) and his law of gravitation. Moreover, since antiquity, most mathematicians were also scientists, and many scientists were also mathematicians. [Nonetheless, the Western tradition of pure mathematics traces its roots back to](#cite_note-125) [Ancient Greece.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_mathematics_in_Ancient_Greece) [The problem of](#cite_note-126) [integer factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_factorization), for example, which goes back to [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid) in 300 BC, had no practical application before its use in the [RSA cryptosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_cryptosystem), now widely used for the security of [computer networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network).

In the 19th century, mathematicians such as [Karl Weierstrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Weierstrass) and [Richard Dedekind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dedekind) increasingly focused their research on internal problems, that is, pure mathematics. [This led to split mathematics into pure mathematics and applied mathematics, the latter being often considered as having a lower value among mathematical purists. However, the lines between the two are frequently blurred.](#cite_note-128)

The aftermath of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) led to a surge in the development of applied mathematics in the US and elsewhere. [Many of the theories developed for applications were found interesting from the point of view of pure mathematics, and many results of pure mathematics were shown to have applications outside mathematics; in turn, the study of these applications may give new insights on the "pure theory".](#cite_note-131)

An example of the first case is the [theory of distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_distributions), introduced by [Laurent Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurent_Schwartz) for validating computations done in [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics), which became immediately an important tool of (pure) mathematical analysis. [An example of the second case is the](#cite_note-134) [decidability of the first-order theory of the real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decidability_of_the_first-order_theory_of_the_real_numbers), a problem of pure mathematics that was proved true by [Alfred Tarski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Tarski), with an algorithm that is impossible to [implement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implementation_(computer_science)) because of a computational complexity that is much too high. [For getting an algorithm that can be implemented and can solve systems of polynomial equations and inequalities,](#cite_note-135) [George Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_E._Collins) introduced the [cylindrical algebraic decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_algebraic_decomposition) that became a fundamental tool in [real algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_algebraic_geometry).

In the present day, the distinction between pure and applied mathematics is more a question of personal research aim of mathematicians than a division of mathematics into broad areas. [The Mathematics Subject Classification has a section for "general applied mathematics" but does not mention "pure mathematics".](#cite_note-138) [However, these terms are still used in names of some](#cite_note-MSC-10) [university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University) departments, such as at the [Faculty of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faculty_of_Mathematics,_University_of_Cambridge) at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge).

### Unreasonable effectiveness

The [unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unreasonable_effectiveness_of_mathematics) is a phenomenon that was named and first made explicit by physicist [Eugene Wigner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Wigner). [It is the fact that many mathematical theories (even the "purest") have applications outside their initial object. These applications may be completely outside their initial area of mathematics, and may concern physical phenomena that were completely unknown when the mathematical theory was introduced.](#cite_note-wigner1960-5) [Examples of unexpected applications of mathematical theories can be found in many areas of mathematics.](#cite_note-139)

A notable example is the [prime factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factorization) of natural numbers that was discovered more than 2,000 years before its common use for secure [internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet) communications through the [RSA cryptosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_cryptosystem). [A second historical example is the theory of](#cite_note-140) [ellipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse). They were studied by the [ancient Greek mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_mathematicians) as [conic sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_section) (that is, intersections of [cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone) with planes). It was almost 2,000 years later that [Johannes Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Kepler) discovered that the [trajectories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectories) of the planets are ellipses.

In the 19th century, the internal development of geometry (pure mathematics) led to definition and study of non-Euclidean geometries, spaces of dimension higher than three and [manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold). At this time, these concepts seemed totally disconnected from the physical reality, but at the beginning of the 20th century, [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein) developed the [theory of relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_relativity) that uses fundamentally these concepts. In particular, [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime) of [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity) is a non-Euclidean space of dimension four, and spacetime of [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity) is a (curved) manifold of dimension four.

A striking aspect of the interaction between mathematics and physics is when mathematics drives research in physics. This is illustrated by the discoveries of the [positron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron) and the [baryon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omega_baryon) In both cases, the equations of the theories had unexplained solutions, which led to conjecture of the existence of an unknown [particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle), and the search for these particles. In both cases, these particles were discovered a few years later by specific experiments.

### Specific sciences

#### Physics

Mathematics and physics have influenced each other over their modern history. Modern physics uses mathematics abundantly, [and is also considered to be the motivation of major mathematical developments.](#cite_note-147)

#### Computing

Computing is closely related to mathematics in several ways.[Theoretical computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_computer_science) is considered to be mathematical in nature. [Communication technologies apply branches of mathematics that may be very old (e.g., arithmetic), especially with respect to transmission security, in](#cite_note-150) [cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptography) and [coding theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coding_theory). [Discrete mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_mathematics) is useful in many areas of computer science, such as [complexity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_complexity_theory), [information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory), and [graph theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_theory). [In 1998, the](#cite_note-151) [Kepler conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler_conjecture) on [sphere packing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere_packing) seemed to also be partially proven by computer.

#### Statistics and other decision sciences

The field of statistics is a mathematical application that is employed for the collection and processing of data samples, using procedures based on mathematical methods such as, and especially, [probability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory). Statisticians generate data with [random sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_sampling) or randomized [experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_of_experiments).

[Statistical theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_theory) studies [decision problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_decision_theory) such as minimizing the [risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk) ([expected loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_loss)) of a statistical action, such as using a [procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_method) in, for example, [parameter estimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameter_estimation), [hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis_testing), and [selecting the best](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selection_algorithm). In these traditional areas of [mathematical statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_statistics), a statistical-decision problem is formulated by minimizing an [objective function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objective_function), like expected loss or [cost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost), under specific constraints. For example, designing a survey often involves minimizing the cost of estimating a population mean with a given level of confidence. [Because of its use of](#cite_note-RaoOpt-155) [optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization), the mathematical theory of statistics overlaps with other [decision sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_science), such as [operations research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operations_research), [control theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_theory), and [mathematical economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_economics).

#### Biology and chemistry

[Biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology) uses probability extensively in fields such as ecology or [neurobiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurobiology). [Most discussion of probability centers on the concept of](#cite_note-:2-157) [evolutionary fitness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_fitness). [Ecology heavily uses modeling to simulate](#cite_note-:2-157) [population dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_dynamics), [study ecosystems such as the predator-prey model, measure pollution diffusion,](#cite_note-158) [or to assess climate change.](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBouleau1999282–283-159) [The dynamics of a population can be modeled by coupled differential equations, such as the](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBouleau1999285-160) [Lotka–Volterra equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotka–Volterra_equations).

[Statistical hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing) is run on data from [clinical trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_trial) to determine whether a new treatment works. [Since the start of the 20th century, chemistry has used computing to model molecules in three dimensions.](#cite_note-162)

#### Earth sciences

[Structural geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_geology) and climatology use probabilistic models to predict the risk of natural catastrophes. [Similarly,](#cite_note-164) [meteorology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorology), [oceanography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanography), and [planetology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetology) also use mathematics due to their heavy use of models.

#### Social sciences

Areas of mathematics used in the social sciences include probability/statistics and differential equations. These are used in [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology), [and](#cite_note-168) [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology).

Often the fundamental postulate of mathematical economics is that of the rational individual actor – [Homo economicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_economicus) (lit.'economic man'). [In this model, the individual seeks to maximize their](#cite_note-:3-170) [self-interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_choice_theory), [and always makes optimal choices using](#cite_note-:3-170) [perfect information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_information). [This atomistic view of economics allows it to relatively easily mathematize its thinking, because individual](#cite_note-171) [calculations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculations) are transposed into mathematical calculations. Such mathematical modeling allows one to probe economic mechanisms. Some reject or criticize the concept of Homo economicus. Economists note that real people have limited information, make poor choices, and care about fairness and altruism, not just personal gain.

Without mathematical modeling, it is hard to go beyond statistical observations or untestable speculation. Mathematical modeling allows economists to create structured frameworks to test hypotheses and analyze complex interactions. Models provide clarity and precision, enabling the translation of theoretical concepts into quantifiable predictions that can be tested against real-world data.

At the start of the 20th century, there was a development to express historical movements in formulas. In 1922, [Nikolai Kondratiev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Kondratiev) discerned the ~50-year-long [Kondratiev cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kondratiev_cycle), which explains phases of economic growth or crisis. [Towards the end of the 19th century, mathematicians extended their analysis into](#cite_note-174) [geopolitics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geopolitics).[Peter Turchin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Turchin) developed [cliodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliodynamics) in the 1990s.

Mathematization of the social sciences is not without risk. In the controversial book [Fashionable Nonsense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fashionable_Nonsense) (1997), [Sokal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Sokal) and [Bricmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Bricmont) denounced the unfounded or abusive use of scientific terminology, particularly from mathematics or physics, in the social sciences. [The study of](#cite_note-177) [complex systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_systems) (evolution of unemployment, business capital, demographic evolution of a population, etc.) uses mathematical knowledge. However, the choice of counting criteria, particularly for unemployment, or of models, can be subject to controversy.

## Philosophy

### Reality

The connection between mathematics and material reality has led to philosophical debates since at least the time of [Pythagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoras). The ancient philosopher [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato) argued that abstractions that reflect material reality have themselves a reality that exists outside space and time. As a result, the philosophical view that mathematical objects somehow exist on their own in abstraction is often referred to as [Platonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Platonism). Independently of their possible philosophical opinions, modern mathematicians may be generally considered as Platonists, since they think of and talk of their objects of study as real objects.

[Armand Borel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armand_Borel) summarized this view of mathematics reality as follows, and provided quotations of [G. H. Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy), [Charles Hermite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Hermite), [Henri Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincaré) and [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein) that support his views.

> Something becomes objective (as opposed to "subjective") as soon as we are convinced that it exists in the minds of others in the same form as it does in ours and that we can think about it and discuss it together.
>
> [Because the language of mathematics is so precise, it is ideally suited to defining concepts for which such a consensus exists. In my opinion, that is sufficient to provide us with a feeling of an objective existence, of a reality of mathematics ...](#cite_note-181)

Nevertheless, Platonism and the concurrent views on abstraction do not explain the [unreasonable effectiveness](#Unreasonable_effectiveness) of mathematics (as Platonism assumes mathematics exists independently, but does not explain why it matches reality).

### Proposed definitions

There is no general consensus about the definition of mathematics or its [epistemological status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology)—that is, its place inside knowledge. A great many professional mathematicians take no interest in a definition of mathematics, or consider it undefinable. There is not even consensus on whether mathematics is an art or a science. Some just say, "mathematics is what mathematicians do". [A common approach is to define mathematics by its object of study.](#cite_note-Runge-184)

Aristotle defined mathematics as "the science of quantity" and this definition prevailed until the 18th century. However, Aristotle also noted a focus on quantity alone may not distinguish mathematics from sciences like physics; in his view, abstraction and studying quantity as a property "separable in thought" from real instances set mathematics apart. [In the 19th century, when mathematicians began to address topics—such as infinite sets—which have no clear-cut relation to physical reality, a variety of new definitions were given.](#cite_note-Franklin-189) [With the large number of new areas of mathematics that have appeared since the beginning of the 20th century, defining mathematics by its object of study has become increasingly difficult.](#cite_note-Cajori-190) [For example, in lieu of a definition,](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDevlin2018[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidgUb7CAAAQBAJpgPA3_3]-191) [Saunders Mac Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saunders_Mac_Lane) in [Mathematics, form and function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics,_form_and_function) summarizes the basics of several areas of mathematics, emphasizing their inter-connectedness, and observes:

> the development of Mathematics provides a tightly connected network of formal rules, concepts, and systems. Nodes of this network are closely bound to procedures useful in human activities and to questions arising in science. The transition from activities to the formal Mathematical systems is guided by a variety of general insights and ideas.

Another approach for defining mathematics is to use its methods. For example, an area of study is often qualified as mathematics as soon as one can prove theorems—assertions whose validity relies on a proof, that is, a purely logical deduction.[[](#cite_note-194)[failed verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability)]

### Rigor

Mathematical reasoning requires [rigor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_rigor). This means that the definitions must be absolutely unambiguous and the [proofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_(mathematics)) must be reducible to a succession of applications of [inference rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inference_rule), [without any use of empirical evidence and](#cite_note-195) [intuition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuition). [Rigorous reasoning is not specific to mathematics, but, in mathematics, the standard of rigor is much higher than elsewhere. Despite mathematics'](#cite_note-197) [concision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concision), rigorous proofs can require hundreds of pages to express, such as the 255-page [Feit–Thompson theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feit–Thompson_theorem). [The emergence of](#cite_note-198) [computer-assisted proofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-assisted_proof) has allowed proof lengths to further expand. [The result of this trend is a philosophy of the](#cite_note-200) [quasi-empiricist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-empiricism_in_mathematics) proof that can not be considered infallible, but has a probability attached to it.

The concept of rigor in mathematics dates back to ancient Greece, where their society encouraged logical, deductive reasoning. However, this rigorous approach would tend to discourage exploration of new approaches, such as irrational numbers and concepts of infinity. The method of demonstrating rigorous proof was enhanced in the sixteenth century through the use of symbolic notation. In the 18th century, social transition led to mathematicians earning their keep through teaching, which led to more careful thinking about the underlying concepts of mathematics. This produced more rigorous approaches, while transitioning from geometric methods to algebraic and then arithmetic proofs.

At the end of the 19th century, it appeared that the definitions of the basic concepts of mathematics were not accurate enough for avoiding paradoxes (non-Euclidean geometries and [Weierstrass function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_function)) and contradictions (Russell's paradox). This was solved by the inclusion of axioms with the [apodictic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apodictic) inference rules of mathematical theories; the re-introduction of axiomatic method pioneered by the ancient Greeks. [It results that "rigor" is no more a relevant concept in mathematics, as a proof is either correct or erroneous, and a "rigorous proof" is simply a](#cite_note-Kleiner_1991-9) [pleonasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleonasm). Where a special concept of rigor comes into play is in the socialized aspects of a proof, wherein it may be demonstrably refuted by other mathematicians. After a proof has been accepted for many years or even decades, it can then be considered as reliable.

Nevertheless, the concept of "rigor" may remain useful for teaching to beginners what is a mathematical proof.

## Training and practice

### Education

Mathematics has a remarkable ability to cross cultural boundaries and time periods. As a [human activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_activity), the practice of mathematics has a social side, which includes [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_education), [careers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician), [recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mathematics_awards), [popularization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_mathematics), and so on. In education, mathematics is a core part of the curriculum and forms an important element of the [STEM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STEM) academic disciplines. Prominent careers for professional mathematicians include mathematics teacher or professor, [statistician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistician), [actuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actuary), [financial analyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_analyst), [economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economist), [accountant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accountant), [commodity trader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodity_trader), or [computer consultant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_technology_consulting).

Archaeological evidence shows that instruction in mathematics occurred as early as the second millennium BCE in ancient Babylonia. [Comparable evidence has been unearthed for scribal mathematics training in the](#cite_note-204) [ancient Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Near_East) and then for the [Greco-Roman world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Roman_world) starting around 300 BCE. [The oldest known mathematics textbook is the](#cite_note-205) [Rhind papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhind_papyrus), dated from c.1650 BCE in Egypt. [Due to a scarcity of books, mathematical teachings in ancient India were communicated using memorized](#cite_note-206) [oral tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition) since the [Vedic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_period) (c.1500– c.500 BCE). [In](#cite_note-207) [Imperial China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_China) during the [Tang dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty) (618–907 CE), a mathematics curriculum was adopted for the [civil service exam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_examination) to join the state bureaucracy.

Following the [Dark Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_Ages_(historiography)), mathematics education in Europe was provided by religious schools as part of the [Quadrivium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrivium). The oldest journal addressing instruction in mathematics was [L'Enseignement Mathématique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L'Enseignement_Mathématique), which began publication in 1899. [The Western advancements in science and technology led to the establishment of centralized education systems in many nation-states, with mathematics as a core component—initially for its military applications.](#cite_note-209) [While the content of courses varies, in the present day nearly all countries teach mathematics to students for significant amounts of time.](#cite_note-210)

During school, mathematical capabilities and positive expectations have a strong association with career interest in the field. Extrinsic factors such as feedback motivation by teachers, parents, and peer groups can influence the level of interest in mathematics. [Some students studying mathematics may develop an apprehension or fear about their performance in the subject. This is known as](#cite_note-212) [mathematical anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_anxiety), and is considered the most prominent of the disorders impacting academic performance. Mathematical anxiety can develop due to various factors such as parental and teacher attitudes, social stereotypes, and personal traits. Help to counteract the anxiety can come from changes in instructional approaches, by interactions with parents and teachers, and by tailored treatments for the individual.

### Psychology (aesthetic, creativity and intuition)

The validity of a mathematical theorem relies only on the rigor of its proof, which could theoretically be done automatically by a [computer program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_program). This does not mean that there is no place for creativity in a mathematical work. On the contrary, many important mathematical results (theorems) are solutions of problems that other mathematicians failed to solve, and the invention of a way for solving them may be a fundamental way of the solving process. [An extreme example is](#cite_note-215) [Apery's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apery's_theorem): [Roger Apery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Apery) provided only the ideas for a proof, and the formal proof was given only several months later by three other mathematicians.

Creativity and rigor are not the only psychological aspects of the activity of mathematicians. Some mathematicians can see their activity as a game, more specifically as solving [puzzles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puzzle). [This aspect of mathematical activity is emphasized in](#cite_note-217) [recreational mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recreational_mathematics).

Mathematicians can find an [aesthetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetic) value to mathematics. Like [beauty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beauty), it is hard to define, it is commonly related to elegance, which involves qualities like [simplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicity), [symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry), completeness, and generality. G. H. Hardy in [A Mathematician's Apology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Mathematician's_Apology) expressed the belief that the aesthetic considerations are, in themselves, sufficient to justify the study of pure mathematics. He also identified other criteria such as significance, unexpectedness, and inevitability, which contribute to mathematical aesthetics.[Paul Erdős](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Erdős) expressed this sentiment more ironically by speaking of "The Book", a supposed divine collection of the most beautiful proofs. The 1998 book [Proofs from THE BOOK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofs_from_THE_BOOK), inspired by Erdős, is a collection of particularly succinct and revelatory mathematical arguments. Some examples of particularly elegant results included are Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many prime numbers and the [fast Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_Fourier_transform) for [harmonic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_analysis).

Some feel that to consider mathematics a science is to downplay its artistry and history in the seven traditional [liberal arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_arts). [One way this difference of viewpoint plays out is in the philosophical debate as to whether mathematical results are created (as in art) or discovered (as in science).](#cite_note-220) [The popularity of recreational mathematics is another sign of the pleasure many find in solving mathematical questions.](#cite_note-borel-144)

### Popularization

Popular mathematics is the act of presenting mathematics without technical terms. [Presenting mathematics may be hard since the general public suffers from](#cite_note-230) [mathematical anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_anxiety) and mathematical objects are highly abstract. [However, popular mathematics writing can overcome this by using applications or cultural links.](#cite_note-231) [Despite this, mathematics is rarely the topic of popularization in printed or televised media.](#cite_note-232)

### Awards and prize problems

The most prestigious award in mathematics is the [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal), [established by Canadian](#cite_note-FOOTNOTERiehm2002778–782-234) [John Charles Fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Charles_Fields) in 1936 and awarded every four years (except around [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II_in_Yugoslavia)) to up to four individuals. [It is considered the mathematical equivalent of the](#cite_note-StAndrews-Fields-236) [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize).

Other prestigious mathematics awards include:

- The [Abel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_Prize), instituted in 2002[and first awarded in 2003](#cite_note-238)
- The [Chern Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chern_Medal)for lifetime achievement, introduced in 2009[and first awarded in 2010](#cite_note-240)
- The [AMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Society)[Leroy P. Steele Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_P._Steele_Prize), awarded since 1970
- The [Wolf Prize in Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize_in_Mathematics), also for lifetime achievement,[instituted in 1978](#cite_note-243)

A famous list of 23 [open problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_problem), called "[Hilbert's problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_problems)", was compiled in 1900 by German mathematician David Hilbert. [This list has achieved great celebrity among mathematicians,](#cite_note-:0-245) [and at least thirteen of the problems (depending how some are interpreted) have been solved.](#cite_note-246)

A new list of seven important problems, titled the "[Millennium Prize Problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Prize_Problems)", was published in 2000. Only one of them, the [Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_hypothesis), duplicates one of Hilbert's problems. A solution to any of these problems carries a 1 million dollar reward. [To date, only one of these problems, the](#cite_note-247) [Poincaré conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_conjecture), has been solved, by the Russian mathematician [Grigori Perelman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grigori_Perelman).
