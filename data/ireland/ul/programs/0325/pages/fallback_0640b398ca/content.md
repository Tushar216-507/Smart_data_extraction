Journalism is the production and distribution of reports on events, facts, ideas, and people that constitute the "news of the day" and inform society with a commitment to accuracy and verification. The word, a [noun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noun), applies to the [occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalist) (professional or not), the methods of gathering information, and the organizing literary styles.

The role of journalism varies across countries, as do perceptions of the profession and its status. In some nations, [news](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News) [media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_media) are controlled by the [government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government) and lack independence. [In others,](#cite_note-1) [news media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_media) operate independently of government influence and function as [private enterprises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_enterprises). Legal frameworks also differ, particularly regarding [freedom of speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_speech), [freedom of the press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press), [slander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slander), and [libel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libel). Scholars have also examined journalism’s role in globalization, highlighting its contribution to increasing global interconnectedness.

In recent years, the rise of the [internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet) and [digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_media) media has significantly changed how people access and consume information, with a growing preference for online sources. In some regions, this shift has contributed to the decline or disappearance of traditional print [newspapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newspaper). [The widespread use of the internet and](#cite_note-3) [smartphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smartphones) has transformed the media landscape since the early 21st century, with audiences increasingly accessing [news](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News) through [e-readers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-reader), [mobile devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_devices), and [digital platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_platform_(infrastructure)) rather than traditional formats such as newspapers, magazines, or [television news](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_channel). [News organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_organizations) face ongoing challenges in monetizing [digital content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_content) and adapting print models to evolving consumption patterns. As a result, print revenues have declined more rapidly than digital income streams have grown.

Journalistic conventions vary by country. In the United States, journalism is produced by media organizations or by individuals. Bloggers are often regarded as journalists. The [Federal Trade Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission) requires that bloggers who write about products received as promotional gifts disclose that they received the products for free. This is intended to eliminate conflicts of interest and protect consumers.

In the US, many credible news organizations are [incorporated entities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incorporation_(business)), have editorial boards, and maintain separate editorial and advertising departments. Many credible news organizations, or their employees, often belong to and abide by the ethics of professional organizations such as the [American Society of News Editors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Society_of_News_Editors), the [Society of Professional Journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Professional_Journalists), [Investigative Reporters & Editors, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investigative_Reporters_&_Editors), or the [Online News Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_News_Association). Many news organizations also have their own codes of ethics that guide journalists' professional publications.

When crafting news stories, regardless of the medium, fairness and [media bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_bias) are concerns for journalists. Some stories are intended to represent the author's own opinion; others are more neutral or feature balanced points of view. For instance, according to the [Australian Bureau of Statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Bureau_of_Statistics) (ABS), there are 8,469 journalists in Australia, including editors and those working in radio, television, and print. Research reveals that considering where journalists actually live gives insight into which communities have their stories told firsthand, while others are told by outsiders.

In a traditional print newspaper and its online version, information is organized into sections. This makes clear the distinction between content based on fact and on opinion. In other media, many of these distinctions break down. Readers should pay careful attention to headings and other design elements to ensure that they understand the journalist's intent. Opinion pieces are generally written by regular columnists or appear in a section titled "Op-ed"; these reflect a journalist's own opinions and ideology. [While](#cite_note-7) [feature stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_stories), breaking news, and [hard news](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_news) stories typically make efforts to remove opinion from the copy.

According to [Robert McChesney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_W._McChesney), healthy journalism in a democratic country must provide an opinion of people in power and those who wish to be in power, must include a range of opinions and must regard the informational needs of all people.

Many debates center on whether journalism ethics require journalists to be objective and neutral. Arguments include the fact that journalists produce news within a particular social context and that they are guided by professional codes of ethics and strive to represent all legitimate points of view. Additionally, the ability to render a subject's complex and fluid narrative with sufficient accuracy is sometimes challenged by the time available to spend with subjects, the affordances or constraints of the medium used to tell the story, and the evolving nature of people's identities.

There are several forms of journalism with diverse audiences. Journalism is said to serve the role of a "[fourth estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_estate)", acting as a [watchdog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watchdog_journalism) on the workings of the government. A single publication (such as a newspaper) contains many forms of journalism, each of which may be presented in different formats. Each section of a newspaper, magazine, or website may cater to a different audience.

Some forms include:

- [Access journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Access_journalism)– journalists who self-censor and voluntarily cease speaking about issues that might embarrass their hosts, guests, or powerful politicians or businesspersons.
- [Advocacy journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advocacy_journalism)– writing to advocate particular viewpoints or influence the opinions of the audience.
- [Broadcast journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_journalism)– written or spoken journalism for radio or television

- [Business journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_journalism)– tracks, records, analyzes and interprets the business, economic and financial activities and changes that take place in societies.
- [Citizen journalism – participatory journalism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizen_journalism)
- [Data journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_journalism)– the practice of finding stories in numbers and using numbers to tell stories. Data journalists may use data to support their reporting. They may also report about uses and misuses of data. The American news organization[ProPublica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ProPublica)is known as a pioneer of data journalism.
- [Drone journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drone_journalism)– use of[drones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unmanned_aerial_vehicle)to capture journalistic footage.
- [Global journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_journalism)– journalism that encompasses a global outlook focusing on intercontinental issues.
- [Gonzo journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonzo_journalism)– first championed by[Hunter S. Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter_S._Thompson), gonzo journalism is a "highly personal style of reporting".
- [Interactive journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interactive_journalism)– a type of online journalism that is presented on the web
- [Investigative journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investigative_journalism)– in-depth reporting that uncovers social problems.
- [Long-form journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-form_journalism)– journalism aimed at producing works that capture the reader's attention for an extended period of time
- [Photojournalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photojournalism)– the practice of telling true stories through images
- [Political journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_journalism)– coverage of all aspects of politics and political science
- [Science journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_journalism)- conveys reporting about science to the public
- [Sensor journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensor_journalism)– the use of sensors to support journalistic inquiry
- [Sports journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_journalism)– writing that reports on matters pertaining to sporting topics and competitions
- [Student journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student_journalism)– the practice of journalism by students at an educational institution, often covering topics particularly relevant to the student body
- [Tabloid journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabloid_journalism)– writing that is light-hearted and entertaining. Considered less legitimate than mainstream journalism.
- [Visual journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_journalism)– data such as[maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparison_of_geographic_information_systems_software),[infographics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_information_graphics_software),[data visualization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_and_information_visualization),[tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_(information)),[charts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chart),[diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagram), and[video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_video_editing_software).
- [War journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_journalism)– the covering of wars and armed conflicts
- [Yellow journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_journalism)(or[sensationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensationalism)) – writing which emphasizes exaggerated claims or rumors.

The rise of social media has drastically changed the nature of journalistic reporting, giving rise to so-called [citizen journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizen_journalism). In a 2014 study of journalists in the United States, 40% of participants claimed they rely on social media as a source, with over 20% depending on [microblogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microblogging) to collect facts. [From this, the conclusion can be drawn that breaking news nowadays often stems from user-generated content, including videos and pictures posted online in social media.](#cite_note-:0-15) [However, though 69.2% of the surveyed journalists agreed that social media allowed them to connect to their audience, only 30% thought it had a positive influence on news credibility.](#cite_note-:0-15) [In addition to this, a 2021 study done by](#cite_note-:0-15) [Pew Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) shows that 86% of Americans are getting their news from digital devices.

Consequently, this has led to arguments for reconsidering journalism as a process distributed among many authors, including the socially mediating public, rather than as individual products and articles written by dedicated journalists.

Because of these changes, the credibility ratings of news outlets have reached an all-time low. A 2014 study revealed that only 22% of Americans reported a "great deal" or "quite a lot of confidence" in either television news or newspapers.

While publications reporting the news to the general public in a standardized fashion only began to appear in the 17th century and later, governments as early as [Han dynasty China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty) made use of regularly published news bulletins. [Similar publications were established in the](#cite_note-43) [Republic of Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice) in the 16th century. [These bulletins, however, were intended only for government officials, and thus were not journalistic news publications in the modern sense of the term.](#cite_note-44)

As mass-printing technologies like the [printing press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press) spread, newspapers were established to provide increasingly literate audiences with the news. The first references to privately owned newspaper publishers in China date to the late [Ming dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty) in 1582.[Johann Carolus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Carolus)'s Relation aller Fürnemmen und gedenckwürdigen Historien, published in 1605 in [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg), is often recognized as the first newspaper in Europe.

[Freedom of the press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press) was formally established in Great Britain in 1695, with [Alan Rusbridger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Rusbridger), former editor of [The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian), stating: "Licensing of the press in Britain was abolished in 1695. Remember how the freedoms won here became a model for much of the rest of the world, and be conscious how the world still watches us to see how we protect those freedoms." [The first successful English daily, the](#cite_note-46) [Daily Courant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_Courant), was published from 1702 to 1735. [While journalistic enterprises were started as private ventures in some regions, such as the](#cite_note-47) [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire) and the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire), other countries such as France and [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussia) kept tighter control of the press, treating it primarily as an outlet for government propaganda and subjecting it to uniform censorship. Other governments, such as the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire), were even more distrustful of the journalistic press and effectively banned journalistic publications until the mid-19th century. [As newspaper publication became a more and more established practice, publishers would increase publication to a weekly or daily rate. Newspapers were more heavily concentrated in cities that were centers of trade, such as](#cite_note-48) [Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amsterdam), London, and [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin). The first newspapers in Latin America would be established in the mid-to-late 19th century.

Newspapers played a significant role in mobilizing popular support for the liberal revolutions of the late 18th and 19th centuries. In the [American Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies), newspapers motivated people to revolt against British rule by publishing grievances against the British crown and republishing pamphlets by revolutionaries such as [Thomas Paine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Paine), [while](#cite_note-50) [loyalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loyalist_(American_Revolution)) publications motivated support against the [American Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution). [News publications in the United States would remain proudly and publicly](#cite_note-51) [partisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partisan_(political)) throughout the 19th century. [In France, political newspapers sprang up during the](#cite_note-52) [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution), with [L'Ami du peuple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L'Ami_du_peuple), edited by [Jean-Paul Marat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Paul_Marat), playing a particularly famous role in arguing for the rights of the revolutionary lower classes. [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) would reintroduce strict censorship laws in 1800, but after his reign, print publications would flourish and play an important role in political culture. [As part of the](#cite_note-53) [Revolutions of 1848](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848), radical liberal publications such as the Rheinische Zeitung, Pesti Hírlap, and [Morgenbladet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morgenbladet) would motivate people toward deposing the [aristocratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristocratic) governments of Central Europe. [Other liberal publications played a more moderate role: The Russian Bulletin praised](#cite_note-54) [Alexander II of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_II_of_Russia)'s liberal reforms in the late 19th century, and supported increased political and economic freedoms for peasants as well as the establishment of a [parliamentary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duma) in Russia. [Farther to the left,](#cite_note-55) [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism) and [communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communism) newspapers had wide followings in France, Russia and Germany despite being outlawed by the government.

The Parisian newspapers were largely stagnant after the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War); circulation inched up to six million a day from five million in 1910. The major postwar success story was [Paris Soir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris-Soir); which lacked any political agenda and was dedicated to providing a mix of sensational reporting to aid circulation, and serious articles to build prestige. By 1939 its circulation was over 1.7 million, double that of its nearest rival, the tabloid Le Petit Parisien. In addition to its daily paper Paris Soir sponsored a highly successful women's magazine Marie-Claire. Another magazine [Match](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Match) was modeled after the photojournalism of the American magazine Life.

By 1900, popular journalism in Britain aimed at the largest possible audience, including the working class, had proven a success and made its profits through advertising. The late 19th and early 20th century in the United States saw the advent of media empires controlled by the likes of [William Randolph Hearst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Randolph_Hearst) and [Joseph Pulitzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Pulitzer). Realizing that they could expand their audience by abandoning politically polarized content, thereby making more money from [advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advertisement), American newspapers began to abandon their partisan politics in favor of less political reporting starting around 1900. [Newspapers of this era embraced sensationalized reporting and larger headline typefaces and layouts, a style that would become dubbed "](#cite_note-67)[yellow journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_journalism)". Newspaper publishing became much more heavily professionalized in this era, and issues of writing quality and workroom discipline saw vast improvement. [This era saw the establishment of](#cite_note-68) [freedom of the press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press) as a legal norm, as President [Theodore Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roosevelt) tried and failed to sue newspapers for reporting corruption in his handling of the purchase of the [Panama Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panama_Canal). [Still, critics note that although the government's ability to suppress journalistic speech is heavily limited, the concentration of newspaper (and general media) ownership in the hands of a small number of private business owners leads to other biases in reporting and media self-censorship that benefits the interests of corporations and the government.](#cite_note-69)

In the 1920s in the United States, as newspapers dropped their blatant partisanship in search of new subscribers, political analyst [Walter Lippmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Lippmann) and philosopher [John Dewey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey) debated the role of journalism in a democracy. [Their differing philosophies still characterize an ongoing debate about the role of journalism in society. Lippmann's views prevailed for decades, helping bolster the Progressives' confidence in expert decision-making, with the general public standing by. Radio

[Radio broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_broadcasting) increased in popularity starting in the 1920s, becoming widespread in the 1930s. While most radio programming was oriented toward music, sports, and entertainment, it also broadcast speeches and occasional news. Radio reached the peak of its importance during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), as radio and [newsreels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsreel) were major sources of up-to-date information on the ongoing war. In the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), radio was heavily used by the state to broadcast political speeches by the leadership. These broadcasts would very rarely have any additional editorial content or analysis, setting them apart from modern news reporting. [The radio would however soon be eclipsed by](#cite_note-80) [broadcast television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_television) starting in the 1950s.

Starting in the 1940s, United States broadcast television channels would air 10- to 15-minute segments of news programming one or two times per evening. The era of live TV news coverage would begin in the 1960s with the assassination of [John F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy), broadcast and reported live on a variety of nationally syndicated television channels. During the 60s and 70s, television channels would begin adding regular morning or midday news shows. Starting in 1980 with the establishment of [CNN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNN), news channels began providing 24-hour news coverage, a format which persists through today.

The role and status of journalism, as well as mass media, have undergone changes over the last two decades, alongside advances in digital technology and the publication of news online. This has created a shift in the consumption of print media, as people increasingly get news from [e-readers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-readers), [smartphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smartphones), and other electronic devices. News organizations are challenged to fully monetize their digital wing and improve the context in which they publish in print. Newspapers have seen print revenues sink faster than digital revenues have grown.

Notably, in the American media landscape, [newsrooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsroom) have reduced staff and coverage as traditional media channels, such as television, have grappled with declining audiences. For example, between 2007 and 2012, [CNN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNN) edited its story packages to nearly half their original length. [The compactness in coverage has been linked to broad audience attrition.](#cite_note-State-81) [According to the Pew Research Center, the circulation for U.S. newspapers has fallen sharply in the 21st century.](#cite_note-State-81) [Digital-first,](#cite_note-82) [non-profit newsrooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-profit_journalism) have grown in response to the need for high-quality information that the private sector has been struggling to provide.

The digital era also introduced journalism, whose development is driven by ordinary citizens, with the rise of [citizen journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizen_journalism) made possible by the internet. Using video camera-equipped smartphones, active citizens can now record footage of news events and upload it to channels like YouTube (which is often discovered and used by mainstream news media outlets). News from a variety of online sources, such as [blogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blogs) and other social media, results in a wider range of official and unofficial sources, rather than only traditional media organizations.

A worldwide sample of 27,500 journalists in 67 countries in 2012–2016 produced the following profile:

- 57 percent male;
- Mean age of 38
- Mean years of experience:13
- College degree: 56 percent; graduate degree: 29 percent
- 61 percent specialized in journalism/communications at college
- 62 percent identified as generalists and 23 percent as hard-news beat journalists
- 47 percent were members of a professional association
- 80 percent worked full-time
- 50 percent worked in print, 23 percent in television, 17 percent in radio, and 16 percent online.

While various existing codes have some differences, most share common elements including the principles of – [truthfulness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth), [accuracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accuracy), [objectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalistic_objectivity), [impartiality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonpartisanship), [fairness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice) and public [accountability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accountability) – as these apply to the acquisition of newsworthy information and its subsequent dissemination to the public.

[Bill Kovach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Kovach) and Tom Rosenstiel propose several guidelines for journalists in their book The Elements of Journalism. [Their view is that journalism's first loyalty is to the citizenry and that journalists are thus obliged to tell the truth and must serve as an independent monitor of powerful individuals and institutions within society. In this view, the essence of journalism is to provide citizens with reliable information through the discipline of verification. The values and preferences of the citizens can be determined with](#cite_note-91) [opinion polls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opinion_poll).

Some journalistic Codes of Ethics, notably the European ones, [also include a concern with](#cite_note-93) [discriminatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrimination) references in news based on [race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_(classification_of_human_beings)), religion, [sexual orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_orientation), and physical or mental [disabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disability). [The](#cite_note-97) [Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_Assembly_of_the_Council_of_Europe) approved in 1993 Resolution 1003 on the Ethics of Journalism which recommends journalists to respect the [presumption of innocence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presumption_of_innocence), in particular in cases that are still [sub judice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub_judice).

In the UK, all newspapers are bound by the Code of Practice of the [Independent Press Standards Organisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Press_Standards_Organisation). This includes points like respecting people's privacy and ensuring accuracy. However, the Media Standards Trust has criticized the PCC, claiming it needs to be radically changed to secure the public trust of newspapers.

This is in stark contrast to the media climate prior to the 20th century, where the media market was dominated by smaller newspapers and pamphleteers who usually had an overt and often radical agenda, with no presumption of balance or objectivity. [But scholars and practitioners have also critiqued the use of objectivity in journalism,](#cite_note-99) [with journalist Ramona Martinez arguing that "objectivity is the ideology of the status quo."](#cite_note-101) [Such critiques of objectivity have led to the rise of](#cite_note-102) [advocacy journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advocacy_journalism) or movement journalism.

Because of the pressure on journalists to report news promptly and before their competitors, factual errors occur more frequently than in writing produced and edited under less time pressure. Thus, a typical issue of a major daily newspaper may contain several corrections of articles published the previous day. Perhaps the most famous journalistic mistake caused by time pressure was the [Dewey Defeats Truman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dewey_Defeats_Truman) edition of the [Chicago Daily Tribune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Daily_Tribune), based on early election returns that failed to anticipate the actual result of the 1948 US presidential election.

There are over 242 codes of ethics in journalism that vary across various regions of the world. [The codes of ethics are created through an interaction of different groups of people such as the public and journalists themselves. Most codes of ethics represent the economic and political beliefs of the society in which they were written.](#cite_note-:02-103) [Despite the fact that there are a variety of codes of ethics, some of the core elements present in all codes are: remaining objective, providing the truth, and being honest.](#cite_note-:02-103)

Journalism does not have a universal [code of conduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_conduct); individuals are not legally obliged to follow a set of rules as a doctor or lawyer is. [There have been discussions for creating a universal code of conduct in journalism. One suggestion centers on having three claims for](#cite_note-104) [credibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credibility), justifiable consequence, and the claim of [humanity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanity_(virtue)). [Within the claim of credibility, journalists are expected to provide the public with reliable and trustworthy information, and allowing the public to question the nature of the information and its acquisition. The second claim of justifiable consequences centers on weighing the benefits and detriments of a potentially harmful story and acting accordingly. An example of justifiable consequence is exposing a professional with dubious practices; on the other hand, acting within justifiable consequence means writing compassionately about a family in mourning. The third claim is the claim of humanity, which states that journalists are writing for a global population and therefore must serve everyone globally in their work, avoiding smaller loyalties to country, city, etc.](#cite_note-:1-105)

Governments have widely varying policies and practices towards journalists, which control what they can research and write, and what press organizations can publish. Some governments guarantee the freedom of the press, while other nations severely restrict what journalists can research or publish.

Journalists in many nations have privileges that members of the general public do not, including better access to public events, crime scenes and press conferences, as well as extended interviews with public officials, celebrities and others in the public eye.

Journalists who elect to cover [conflicts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War), whether wars between nations or [insurgencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurgency) within nations, often give up any expectation of government protection, if not their rights to such protection. Journalists who are captured or detained during a conflict are expected to be treated as civilians and to be released to their national government. Many governments around the world target journalists for intimidation, harassment, and violence because of the nature of their work.

Journalists' interaction with sources sometimes involves [confidentiality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidentiality), an extension of freedom of the press that gives journalists legal protection to keep the identity of a [confidential informant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidential_informant) private, even when demanded by police or prosecutors; withholding their sources can land journalists in contempt of court or in jail.

In the United States, there is no right to protect sources in a [federal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_government_of_the_United_States) court. However, federal courts will not compel journalists to reveal their sources unless the information the court seeks is highly relevant to the case and there is no other way to obtain it. State courts provide varying degrees of such protection. Journalists who refuse to testify, even when ordered to do so, can be found in [contempt of court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contempt_of_court) and fined or jailed. On the journalistic side of keeping sources confidential, there is also a risk to the journalist's credibility, as there is no way to verify whether the information is valid. As such it is highly discouraged for journalists to have confidential sources
