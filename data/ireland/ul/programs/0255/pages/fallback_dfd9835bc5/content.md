# Ireland

- What is Ireland?

  Ireland is an island nation to the west of Great Britain. The island is divided into two parts: the Republic of Ireland, a sovereign country, and Northern Ireland, which is part of the United Kingdom. The Republic of Ireland has a population of approximately 5,374,000 as of 2025.

  Irish culture has been shaped by ancient Celtic traditions and external influences, especially from Britain. This has resulted in a distinctive culture where language, whether

  [Irish](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Irish-language)or English, is central. The country is known for its literature, drama, music, and the art of conversation. The Irish pub is a focal point in many communities, fostering socializing, music, and storytelling.3 Britannica Sources

- What is the capital city of Ireland?

  The capital of Ireland is

  [Dublin](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland). Situated on the east coast in the province of Leinster, Dublin is not only the country’s chief port but also a center of financial and commercial power and a seat of culture.

  From its early habitation 2,000 years ago to its establishment as a Norse settlement in the 9th century, Dublin has grown into a city of contrasts, with reminders of its political and economic past alongside modern life and prosperity. Dublin's rich cultural history is reflected in its architecture,

- What language do most people speak in Ireland?

  In Ireland, both Irish and English are official languages. English is spoken by most of the population. Although Irish is the national language, its use declined significantly in the 19th century.

  Today, Irish is a compulsory subject in schools and is required for some civil service positions. While there are efforts to revive and preserve the Irish language, the majority of the population uses English in their daily lives.
- What are some important symbols or traditions of Ireland?

  Ireland has a vibrant cultural heritage with unique symbols and traditions. The

  [shamrock](https://www.britannica.com/plant/shamrock/Introduction), a trifoliate plant, is a national emblem associated with[St. Patrick](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Saint-Patricks-Day/Introduction), the patron saint of Ireland, who used it to explain the Trinity. The[flag of Ireland](https://www.britannica.com/topic/flag-of-Ireland/Introduction#ref1)features green, white, and orange stripes, symbolizing Catholics, Protestants, and peace between them.

  Traditional Irish music, often performed in pubs, involves instruments like the fiddle, tin whistle, and

  [uilleann pipes](https://www.britannica.com/art/stringed-instrument/The-development-of-stringed-instruments#ref53720)(Irish bagpipes). The[Celtic harp](https://www.britannica.com/art/stringed-instrument/The-development-of-stringed-instruments#ref53720)is another emblem of Ireland. - How has Ireland's history influenced its culture and identity today?

  Ireland's history has significantly shaped its culture and identity today. The dynamic interplay between ancient Celtic traditions and external influences, especially from Britain, has produced a distinctive culture where language, whether

  [Irish or English](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland/Music-and-dance#ref23041), is central.

  The

  [Irish language](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland/Ethnic-groups-language-and-religion#ref234218)faced decline but experienced a resurgence, inspiring the Irish literary renaissance and the nationalist struggle of the early 20th century. Traditional[music and dance](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland/Music-and-dance#ref23041)also saw revivals, contributing to a unique Irish popular music form with international appeal. Despite invasions and colonization by various groups, Ireland has maintained a welcoming culture, with[diversity](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland/Ethnic-groups-language-and-religion#ref234218)as a long-standing characteristic.

## News •

Ireland, [country](https://www.britannica.com/topic/nation-state) of western [Europe](https://www.britannica.com/place/Europe) occupying five-sixths of the westernmost major island of the [British Isles](https://www.britannica.com/place/British-Isles).

The magnificent scenery of Ireland’s [Atlantic](https://www.britannica.com/place/Atlantic-Ocean) coastline faces a 2,000-mile- (3,200-km-) wide expanse of ocean, and its geographic isolation has helped it to develop a rich heritage of [culture](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/culture) and tradition that was linked initially to the [Gaelic language](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Irish-language). Washed by abundant rain, the country’s [pervasive](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/pervasive) grasslands create a green-hued landscape that is responsible for the popular [sobriquet](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/sobriquet) Emerald Isle. Ireland is also renowned for its wealth of folklore, from tales of tiny leprechauns with hidden pots of [gold](https://www.britannica.com/science/gold-chemical-element) to that of the [patron saint](https://www.britannica.com/topic/patron-saint), [Patrick](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Saint-Patrick), with his legendary ridding the island of snakes and his reputed use of the three-leaved [shamrock](https://www.britannica.com/plant/shamrock) as a symbol for the Christian [Trinity](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Trinity-Christianity). But while many may think of Ireland as an enchanted land, the republic has been beset with [perennial](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/perennial) concerns—emigration, cultural and political identity, and relations with [Northern Ireland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Northern-Ireland) (comprising the 6 of Ireland’s 32 counties within the province of [Ulster](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ulster-historic-province-Ireland) that remain part of the [United Kingdom](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-Kingdom)). At the beginning of the 21st century, Ireland’s long-standing economic problems were abating, owing to its [diverse](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/diverse) export-driven economy, but [calamity](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/calamity) struck again in 2008 when a new financial and economic crisis befell the country, culminating in a very costly bailout of the Irish economy by the [European Union](https://www.britannica.com/topic/European-Union) (EU) and the [International Monetary Fund](https://www.britannica.com/topic/International-Monetary-Fund).

The emergence of Ireland as an independent country is a fairly recent phenomenon. Until the 17th century, political power was widely shared among a rather loosely constructed network of small earldoms in often-shifting alliances. Following the so-called “Flight of the Earls” after an unsuccessful uprising in the early 17th century, Ireland effectively became an English colony. It was formally incorporated into the United Kingdom in 1801. A 1914 [Home Rule](https://www.britannica.com/event/Home-Rule-Great-Britain-and-Ireland) Act was passed but never [implemented](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/implemented) due to pro-union militancy in the north, the onset of [World War I](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-I), and the subsequent [Irish War of Independence](https://www.britannica.com/event/Irish-War-of-Independence). In 1920 the island was effectively partitioned with the creation of [Northern Ireland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Northern-Ireland), a six-county area with devolved powers within the United Kingdom, whereas under the [Anglo-Irish Treaty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Anglo-Irish-Treaty) of December 6, 1921, the other 26 counties became the Irish Free State, a self-governing [dominion](https://www.britannica.com/topic/dominion-British-Commonwealth) within the British [Commonwealth](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Commonwealth-association-of-states) and [Empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/British-Empire). In 1937 the southern state passed a new constitution that offered a more [robust](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/robust) expression of [sovereignty](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/sovereignty), and in 1949 it formally left the Commonwealth as the Republic of Ireland.

Since then Ireland has become [integrated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/integrated) with the rest of western Europe. It joined the [European Economic Community](https://www.britannica.com/topic/European-Community-European-economic-association) (forerunner of the EU) in 1973. Though the country generally retained a neutral role in international affairs, in 2008 Ireland became an impediment to the enactment of the [Lisbon Treaty](https://www.britannica.com/event/Lisbon-Treaty)—an agreement aimed at streamlining the EU’s processes and giving it a higher international profile—when the Irish voted against the passage of the treaty in a national referendum. The treaty, however, was approved by Irish voters in a second referendum, held the following year.

Dependent on agriculture, Ireland was long among Europe’s poorest regions, a principal cause of mass migration from Ireland, especially during the cycle of famine in the 19th century. Some 40 million Americans trace their ancestry to Ireland as a result of that traumatic exodus, as do millions of others throughout the world. Every year members of this [diaspora](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/diaspora) visit their ancestral homeland and forge connections with long-lost family.

## Land

The republic of Ireland occupies the greater part of an island lying to the west of Great Britain, from which it is separated—at distances ranging from 11 to 120 miles (18 to 193 km)—by the [North Channel](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-Channel-strait), the [Irish Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Irish-Sea), and [St. George’s](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Saint-George) Channel. Located in the temperate zone between latitudes 51°30′ and 55°30′ N and longitudes 6°00′ and 10°30′ W—as far north as [Labrador](https://www.britannica.com/place/Labrador) or [British Columbia](https://www.britannica.com/place/British-Columbia) in [Canada](https://www.britannica.com/place/Canada) and as far west as the West African state of [Liberia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Liberia)—it [constitutes](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitutes) the westernmost outpost of the Atlantic fringe of the Eurasian landmass (the absolute extreme of which is Tearaght Island, the westernmost member of the Blasket Islands, which lie off the [Dingle](https://www.britannica.com/place/Dingle-Peninsula) Peninsula and are part of [County Kerry](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kerry)). Ireland, which, like Great Britain, once formed part of this landmass, lies on the European [continental shelf](https://www.britannica.com/science/continental-shelf), surrounded by seas that are generally less than 650 feet (200 metres) deep. The greatest distance from north to south in the island is 302 miles (486 km), and from east to west it is 171 miles (275 km).

## Relief

The territory of the republic consists of a broad and undulating central [plain](https://www.britannica.com/science/plain) underlain by [limestone](https://www.britannica.com/science/limestone). This plain is ringed almost completely by coastal highlands, which vary considerably in geologic structure. The flatness of the central lowland—which lies for the most part between 200 and 400 feet (60 and 120 metres) above sea level—is relieved in many places by low hills between 600 and 1,000 feet (180 to 300 metres) in elevation. With many lakes, large bog areas, and low ridges, the lowland is very scenic. The principal mountain ranges are the Blue Stack Mountains in the north, the [Wicklow Mountains](https://www.britannica.com/place/Wicklow-Mountains) in the east (topped by [Lugnaquillia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lugnaquillia-Mountain), at 3,039 feet [926 metres]), the Knockmealdown and Comeragh mountains in the south, the Macgillycuddy’s [Reeks](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/Reeks) in the southwest, and the Twelve Pins in the west. [Carrantuohill](https://www.britannica.com/place/Carrantuohill), at 3,414 feet (1,041 metres) in the [Macgillycuddy’s Reeks](https://www.britannica.com/place/Macgillycuddys-Reeks), is the highest point in the republic. In the west and southwest the wild and beautiful coast is heavily indented where the mountains of [Donegal](https://www.britannica.com/place/Donegal-county-Ireland), [Mayo](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mayo-county-Ireland), [Galway](https://www.britannica.com/place/Galway-county-Ireland), and Kerry thrust out into the Atlantic, separated by deep wide-mouthed bays, some of which—[Bantry Bay](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bantry-Bay) and [Dingle Bay](https://www.britannica.com/place/Dingle-Bay), for example—are, in fact, drowned river valleys. By contrast, the east coast is little indented, but most of the country’s trade passes through its ports because of their proximity to British and Continental markets.

The coastal mountain fringe illustrates the country’s complex geologic history. In the west and northwest as well as in the east, the mountains are composed mainly of granite. [Old Red Sandstone](https://www.britannica.com/science/Old-Red-Sandstone) predominates in the south, where the parallel folded mountain ridges trend east-west, separated by limestone river valleys. Ireland experienced at least two general [glaciations](https://www.britannica.com/science/glaciation)—one covering most of the country and the other extending as far south as a line linking [Limerick](https://www.britannica.com/place/Limerick-county-Ireland), [Cashel](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cashel), and Dublin—and the characteristic [diversity](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/diversity) of Irish scenery owes much to this glacial influence. The large areas of [peat](https://www.britannica.com/technology/peat) bog to be found throughout the country are a notable feature of the landscape.
