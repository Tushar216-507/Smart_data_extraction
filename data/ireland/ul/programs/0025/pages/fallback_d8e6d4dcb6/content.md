# Europe

- What is Europe?[Europe](https://www.britannica.com/place/Europe/Introduction#ref1)is the second smallest continent, forming the western peninsulas of Eurasia and covering approximately one-fifteenth of the world's land area. It is bounded by the Arctic Ocean to the north, the Atlantic Ocean to the west, and the Mediterranean, Black, and Caspian Seas to the south. The eastern boundary extends from the Ural Mountains to the northern Caspian coast.

  Europe has functioned as a distinct civilization since it emerged in human consciousness, characterized by a relatively mild environment and a long history. The continent's modern economy evolved from advancements in agriculture, industrial growth, and specialized services, propelled by abundant resources, innovation, and a skilled workforce.

- How has Europe's history shaped its culture?

  Europe's history has significantly shaped its culture through various factors. The

  [Roman Empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Rome)spread its civilization across Europe, influencing language, law, and state organization. Christianity, which gained prominence under Roman rule, introduced new elements of equality and unification while also integrating Middle Eastern and Hellenistic cultures into the West.

  The

  [French Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/French-Revolution)in 1789 sparked cultural nationalism, emphasizing unique national cultures and histories. This era saw the rise of vernacular literature and a focus on folk traditions, influencing artistic expression. The subsequent world wars in the 20th century led to a sense of shared identity and a push for European unity, impacting social, economic, and political structures.

Europe, second smallest of the world’s [continents](https://www.britannica.com/science/continent), composed of the westward-projecting peninsulas of [Eurasia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Eurasia) (the great landmass that it shares with [Asia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Asia)) and occupying nearly one-fifteenth of the world’s total land area. It is bordered on the north by the [Arctic Ocean](https://www.britannica.com/place/Arctic-Ocean), on the west by the [Atlantic Ocean](https://www.britannica.com/place/Atlantic-Ocean), and on the south (west to east) by the [Mediterranean Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mediterranean-Sea), the [Black Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Black-Sea), the [Kuma-Manych Depression](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kuma-Manych-Depression), and the [Caspian Sea](https://www.britannica.com/place/Caspian-Sea). The [continent’s](https://www.britannica.com/science/continent) eastern boundary (north to south) runs along the [Ural Mountains](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ural-Mountains) and then roughly southwest along the Emba (Zhem) River, terminating at the northern Caspian coast.

Europe’s largest islands and archipelagoes include [Novaya Zemlya](https://www.britannica.com/place/Novaya-Zemlya), [Franz Josef Land](https://www.britannica.com/place/Franz-Josef-Land), [Svalbard](https://www.britannica.com/place/Svalbard), [Iceland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iceland), the [Faroe Islands](https://www.britannica.com/place/Faroe-Islands-Atlantic-Ocean), the [British Isles](https://www.britannica.com/place/British-Isles), the [Balearic Islands](https://www.britannica.com/place/Balearic-Islands), [Corsica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Corsica), [Sardinia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sardinia-island-Italy), [Sicily](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sicily), [Malta](https://www.britannica.com/place/Malta), [Crete](https://www.britannica.com/place/Crete), and [Cyprus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cyprus). Its major peninsulas include [Jutland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Jutland) and the [Scandinavian](https://www.britannica.com/place/Scandinavian-Peninsula), [Iberian](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iberian-Peninsula), [Italian](https://www.britannica.com/place/Italian-Peninsula), and [Balkan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Balkans) peninsulas. Indented by numerous bays, fjords, and seas, continental Europe’s highly irregular coastline is about 24,000 miles (38,000 km) long.

Among the continents, Europe is an [anomaly](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/anomaly). Larger only than [Australia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Australia), it is a small appendage of Eurasia. Yet the peninsular and insular western extremity of the continent, thrusting toward the North Atlantic Ocean, provides—thanks to its latitude and its physical geography—a relatively genial human habitat, and the long processes of human history came to mark off the region as the home of a distinctive civilization. In spite of its internal [diversity](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/diversity), Europe has thus functioned, from the time it first emerged in the human [consciousness](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/consciousness), as a world apart, concentrating—to borrow a phrase from [Christopher Marlowe](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christopher-Marlowe)—“infinite riches in a little room.”

As a [conceptual](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/conceptual) construct, Europa, as the more learned of the [ancient Greeks](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Greece) first conceived it, stood in sharp contrast to both [Asia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Asia-ancient-Roman-province) and Libya, the name then applied to the known northern part of [Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Africa). Literally, [Europa](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Europa-Greek-mythology) is now thought to have meant “Mainland,” rather than the earlier interpretation, “Sunset.” It appears to have suggested itself to the Greeks, in their maritime world, as an appropriate [designation](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/designation) for the extensive northerly lands that lay beyond, lands with characteristics vaguely known yet clearly different from those [inherent](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/inherent) in the concepts of Asia and Libya—both of which, relatively prosperous and civilized, were associated closely with the [culture](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/culture) of the Greeks and their predecessors. From the Greek perspective then, Europa was culturally backward and scantily settled. It was a barbarian world—that is, a non-Greek one, with its inhabitants making “bar-bar” noises in unintelligible tongues. Traders and travelers also reported that the Europe beyond [Greece](https://www.britannica.com/place/Greece) possessed distinctive physical units, with mountain systems and lowland river basins much larger than those familiar to inhabitants of the Mediterranean region. It was clear as well that a succession of climates, markedly different from those of the Mediterranean borderlands, were to be experienced as Europe was penetrated from the south. The spacious eastern steppes and, to the west and north, primeval forests as [yet](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/yet) only marginally touched by human occupancy further underlined environmental contrasts.

The empire of [ancient Rome](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Rome), at its greatest extent in the 2nd century ce, revealed, and imprinted its culture on, much of the face of the continent. Trade relations beyond its frontiers also drew the remoter regions into its sphere. Yet it was not until the 19th and 20th centuries that modern [science](https://www.britannica.com/science/science) was able to draw with some precision the geologic and geographic lineaments of the European continent, the peoples of which had meanwhile achieved domination over—and set in motion vast countervailing movements among—the inhabitants of much of the rest of the globe (see [Western colonialism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Western-colonialism)).

As to the territorial limits of Europe, they may seem relatively clear on its seaward flanks, but many island groups far to the north and west—Svalbard, the Faroes, Iceland, and the [Madeira](https://www.britannica.com/place/Madeira-Islands) and [Canary](https://www.britannica.com/place/Canary-Islands) islands—are considered European, while [Greenland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Greenland) (though tied politically to Denmark) is conventionally [allocated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/allocated) to [North America](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-America). Furthermore, the Mediterranean coastlands of [North Africa](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-Africa) and southwestern Asia also exhibit some European physical and cultural [affinities](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/affinities). [Turkey](https://www.britannica.com/place/Turkey) and [Cyprus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cyprus) in particular, while geologically Asian, possess elements of European culture and may be regarded as parts of Europe. Indeed, Turkey has sought membership in the [European Union](https://www.britannica.com/topic/European-Union) (EU), and the Republic of Cyprus joined the organization in 2004.

Europe’s boundaries have been especially uncertain, and hence much debated, on the east, where the continent merges, without sundering physical boundaries, with parts of western Asia. The eastward limits now adopted by most geographers exclude the [Caucasus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Caucasus) region and [encompass](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/encompass) a small portion of [Kazakhstan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kazakhstan), where the European boundary formed by the northern [Caspian](https://www.britannica.com/place/Caspian-Sea) coast is connected to that of the Urals by Kazakhstan’s Emba River and Mughalzhar (Mugodzhar) Hills, themselves a southern extension of the Urals. Among the [alternative](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/alternative) boundaries proposed by geographers that have gained wide acceptance is a scheme that sees the crest of the [Greater Caucasus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Greater-Caucasus) range as the dividing line between Europe and Asia, placing [Ciscaucasia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ciscaucasia), the northern part of the Caucasus region, in Europe and [Transcaucasia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Transcaucasia), the southern part, in Asia. Another widely [endorsed](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/endorsed) scheme puts the western portion of the Caucasus region in Europe and the eastern part—that is, the bulk of [Azerbaijan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Azerbaijan) and small portions of [Armenia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Armenia), [Georgia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Georgia), and Russia’s Caspian Sea coast—in Asia. Still another scheme with many adherents locates the continental boundary along the [Aras River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Aras-River) and the Turkish border, thereby putting Armenia, Azerbaijan, and Georgia in Europe.

Europe’s eastern boundary, however, is not a cultural, political, or economic discontinuity on the land comparable, for example, to the insulating significance of the [Himalayas](https://www.britannica.com/place/Himalayas), which clearly mark a northern limit to South Asian civilization. Inhabited plains, with only the minor interruption of the worn-down Urals, extend from central Europe to the [Yenisey River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Yenisey-River) in central [Siberia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Siberia). Slavic-based civilization dominates much of the territory occupied by the former [Soviet Union](https://www.britannica.com/place/Soviet-Union) from the Baltic and Black seas to the [Pacific Ocean](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pacific-Ocean). That civilization is distinguished from the rest of Europe by [legacies](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/legacies) of a [medieval](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/medieval) [Mongol](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Mongol)-[Tatar](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tatar) domination that precluded the sharing of many of the [innovations](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/innovations) and developments of European “Western civilization”; it became further distinctive during the relative isolation of the Soviet period. In partitioning the globe into meaningful large geographic units, therefore, most modern geographers treated the former Soviet Union as a distinct territorial entity, comparable to a continent, that was somewhat separate from Europe to the west and from Asia to the south and east; that distinction has been maintained for [Russia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Russia), which [constituted](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constituted) three-fourths of the Soviet Union.

Europe occupies some 4 million square miles (10 million square km) within the conventional borders assigned to it. That broad territory reveals no simple unity of geologic structure, landform, relief, or climate. Rocks of all geologic periods are exposed, and the operation of geologic forces during an immense succession of eras has contributed to the molding of the landscapes of mountain, plateau, and lowland and has [bequeathed](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/bequeathed) a variety of mineral reserves. Glaciation too has left its mark over wide areas, and the processes of erosion and [deposition](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/deposition) have created a highly variegated and compartmentalized countryside. Climatically, Europe benefits by having only a small proportion of its surface either too cold or too hot and dry for effective settlement and use. Regional climatic contrasts nevertheless exist: oceanic, Mediterranean, and continental types occur widely, as do gradations from one to the other. Associated vegetation and soil forms also show continual variety, but only portions of the dominant woodland that clothed most of the continent when humans first appeared now remain.

All in all, Europe enjoys a considerable and long-exploited resource base of soil, [forest](https://www.britannica.com/science/forest), sea, and minerals (notably coal), but its people are increasingly its principal resource. The continent, [excluding](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/excluding) Russia, contains less than one-tenth of the total population of the world, but in general its people are well educated and highly skilled. Europe also supports high densities of population, concentrated in urban-industrial regions. A growing percentage of people in urban areas are employed in a wide range of service activities, which have come to dominate the economies of most countries. Nonetheless, in manufacturing and agriculture Europe still occupies an eminent, if no longer necessarily predominant, position. The creation of the [European Economic Community](https://www.britannica.com/topic/European-Community-European-economic-association) in 1957 and the EU in 1993 greatly [enhanced](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/enhanced) economic cooperation between many of the continent’s countries. Europe’s continuing economic achievements are evidenced by its high [standard of living](https://www.britannica.com/money/standard-of-living) and its successes in science, technology, and the arts.
