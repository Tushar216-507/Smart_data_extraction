# psychology

- What is psychology?[Psychology](https://www.britannica.com/science/psychology/Introduction#ref1)is a science that explores mental states, cognitive processes, and behaviors in both humans and animals. The discipline is divided between a science of the mind, brain, and social behavior and a professional practice; some psychologists integrate the two.

  The word "psychology" combines the Greek words psychē, meaning "soul," and -logia, referring to "speech" or "reason". Contemporary psychology is concerned with the science of mind and behavior, with branches including animal, child, and

  [sports psychology](https://www.britannica.com/science/applied-psychology/Introduction#ref1).
- What do psychologists study?

  Psychology is a science that studies mental and biological processes and behavior in humans and other animals. It utilizes diverse research methods to study verbal and nonverbal behavior and mental processes. These methods include questionnaires, interviews, direct observation, and tests of personality, attitudes, and intelligence.

  Psychologists work in various subfields, such as

  [clinical](https://www.britannica.com/science/clinical-psychology/Introduction#ref1),[developmental](https://www.britannica.com/science/developmental-psychology/Introduction#ref1),[social](https://www.britannica.com/science/psychology/Linking-mind-brain-and-behaviour#ref288099), and[cognitive psychology](https://www.britannica.com/topic/cognition-thought-process/Introduction#ref1), using different methods and tools that draw on multiple levels of analysis, from the social to the cognitive and to the biological. Clinical psychologists classify their activities under assessment, treatment, and research. Developmental psychologists study changes in cognitive, motivational, psychophysiological, and social functioning throughout a human lifespan.6 Britannica Sources
- How do psychologists use experiments to understand behavior?

  Psychologists use experiments to understand behavior by manipulating variables and observing outcomes in controlled environments. Experimental design involves creating experimental and control groups. The experimental group receives a treatment, while the control group does not, allowing researchers to determine if the treatment has a significant effect. Random assignment of participants helps ensure that individual characteristics are evenly distributed across groups. Methods like questionnaires, interviews, and direct observation are employed to study behavior and mental processes.

- What is the difference between thoughts, feelings, and behaviors in psychology?

  In psychology, thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are interconnected but distinct elements.

  [Thoughts](https://www.britannica.com/topic/thought/Introduction#ref1)are covert symbolic responses to stimuli and mediate between inner activity and external stimuli.[Feelings](https://www.britannica.com/science/cognitive-behavior-therapy/Introduction#ref1)are a person's emotional state or reaction. Behaviors are observable actions or reactions to internal or external stimuli.[Cognitive behavior therapy (CBT)](https://www.britannica.com/science/cognitive-behavior-therapy/Introduction#ref1)utilizes the relationship between these concepts. CBT focuses on how thinking patterns affect emotions and behaviors. By changing these patterns, individuals can better manage their emotions and alter their behaviors to deal more realistically and effectively with the problems and stresses in their lives.
- How do psychologists help people with mental health problems?

  Psychologists employ various methods to support individuals facing mental health challenges.

  [Clinical psychologists](https://www.britannica.com/science/clinical-psychology/Introduction#ref1)use assessments like psychological tests and interviews to diagnose mental disorders.

  For treatment, they provide

  [psychotherapy](https://www.britannica.com/science/psychotherapy/Introduction#ref1), utilizing techniques such as cognitive behavior therapy to modify negative thinking patterns and promote healthy behaviors. Some psychologists may specialize in group, family, or behavior therapy, tailoring their approach to the client's needs. They may work in hospitals, clinics, and private practices, assisting diverse populations, including those with mental or physical conditions.5 Britannica Sources

psychology, scientific [discipline](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/discipline) that studies mental states and processes and [behaviour](https://www.britannica.com/topic/human-behavior) in humans and other animals.

The discipline of psychology is broadly divisible into two parts: a large profession of practitioners and a smaller but growing [science](https://www.britannica.com/science/science) of [mind](https://www.britannica.com/topic/mind), [brain](https://www.britannica.com/science/brain), and social behaviour. ## Early history

In Western [culture](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/culture), contributors to the development of psychology came from many areas, beginning with philosophers such as [Plato](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Plato) and [Aristotle](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Aristotle). [Hippocrates](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hippocrates) philosophized about basic human [temperaments](https://www.britannica.com/topic/temperament) (e.g., choleric, [sanguine](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/sanguine), melancholic) and their associated traits. Informed by the [biology](https://www.britannica.com/science/biology) of his time, he speculated that physical qualities, such as yellow bile or too much blood, might underlie differences in temperament (see also [humour](https://www.britannica.com/topic/humor)). Aristotle postulated the brain to be the seat of the rational human mind, and in the 17th century [René Descartes](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Rene-Descartes) argued that the mind gives people the capacities for [thought](https://www.britannica.com/topic/thought) and [consciousness](https://www.britannica.com/topic/consciousness): the mind “decides” and the body carries out the decision—a [dualistic](https://www.britannica.com/topic/mind-body-dualism) mind-body split that modern psychological science is still working to overcome. Two figures who helped to found psychology as a formal discipline and science in the 19th century were [Wilhelm Wundt](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Wilhelm-Wundt) in Germany and [William James](https://www.britannica.com/biography/William-James) in the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States). James’s [The Principles of Psychology](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Principles-of-Psychology-by-James) (1890) defined psychology as the science of mental life and provided insightful discussions of topics and challenges that anticipated much of the field’s research agenda a century later.

During the first half of the 20th century, however, [behaviourism](https://www.britannica.com/science/behaviourism-psychology) dominated most of American academic psychology. In 1913 [John B. Watson](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-B-Watson), one of the influential founders of behaviourism, urged reliance on only objectively measurable actions and conditions, effectively removing the study of [consciousness](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/consciousness) from psychology. He argued that psychology as a science must deal exclusively with directly observable behaviour in lower animals as well as humans, emphasized the importance of rewarding only desired behaviours in child rearing, and drew on principles of [learning](https://www.britannica.com/science/learning) through classical [conditioning](https://www.britannica.com/science/conditioning) (based on studies with dogs by the Russian physiologist [Ivan Pavlov](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ivan-Pavlov) and thus known as [Pavlovian conditioning](https://www.britannica.com/science/Pavlovian-conditioning)). In the United States most university psychology departments became devoted to turning psychology away from [philosophy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/philosophy) and into a rigorous [empirical](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/empirical) science.

## Behaviourism

Beginning in the 1930s, behaviourism flourished in the United States, with [B.F. Skinner](https://www.britannica.com/biography/B-F-Skinner) leading the way in demonstrating the power of [operant conditioning](https://www.britannica.com/science/conditioning) through reinforcement. Behaviourists in university settings conducted experiments on the conditions controlling learning and “shaping” behaviour through reinforcement, usually working with [laboratory](https://www.britannica.com/science/laboratory-science) animals such as rats and pigeons. Skinner and his followers explicitly excluded mental life, viewing the human mind as an impenetrable “black box,” open only to conjecture and speculative fictions. Their work showed that social behaviour is readily influenced by manipulating specific [contingencies](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/contingencies) and by changing the consequences or reinforcement (rewards) to which behaviour leads in different situations. Changes in those consequences can modify behaviour in predictable stimulus-response (S-R) patterns. Likewise, a wide range of [emotions](https://www.britannica.com/science/emotion), both positive and negative, may be acquired through processes of conditioning and can be modified by applying the same principles.

## Freud and his followers

Concurrently, in a curious [juxtaposition](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/juxtaposition), the [psychoanalytic](https://www.britannica.com/science/psychoanalysis) theories and therapeutic practices developed by the Vienna-trained physician [Sigmund Freud](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Sigmund-Freud) and his many disciples—beginning early in the 20th century and enduring for many decades—were undermining the traditional view of [human nature](https://www.britannica.com/topic/human-nature) as essentially rational. Freudian theory made [reason](https://www.britannica.com/topic/reason) secondary: for Freud, the [unconscious](https://www.britannica.com/science/unconscious) and its often socially unacceptable irrational motives and desires, particularly the sexual and aggressive, were the driving force underlying much of [human behaviour](https://www.britannica.com/topic/human-behavior) and [mental illness](https://www.britannica.com/science/mental-disorder). Making the unconscious conscious became the therapeutic goal of clinicians working within this framework.

Freud proposed that much of what humans feel, think, and do is outside awareness, self-defensive in its motivations, and unconsciously determined. Much of it also reflects conflicts grounded in early childhood that [play](https://www.britannica.com/topic/play-behavior) out in complex patterns of seemingly paradoxical behaviours and symptoms. His followers, the [ego](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ego-philosophy-and-psychology) psychologists, emphasized the importance of the higher-order functions and [cognitive](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/cognitive) processes (e.g., competence [motivation](https://www.britannica.com/topic/motivation), self-regulatory abilities) as well as the individual’s psychological [defense mechanisms](https://www.britannica.com/topic/defense-mechanism). They also shifted their focus to the roles of interpersonal relations and of secure attachment in [mental health](https://www.britannica.com/science/mental-health) and adaptive functioning, and they pioneered the analysis of these processes in the clinical setting.

## After World War II and [Sputnik](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Sputnik)

After [World War II](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-II), American psychology, particularly clinical psychology, grew into a substantial field in its own right, partly in response to the needs of returning veterans. The growth of psychology as a science was stimulated further by the launching of [Sputnik](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Sputnik) in 1957 and the opening of the Russian-American space race to the [Moon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Moon). As part of this race, the U.S. government fueled the growth of science. For the first time, massive federal funding became available, both to support behavioral research and to enable graduate training. Psychology became both a thriving profession of practitioners and a scientific discipline that investigated all aspects of human social behaviour, [child development](https://www.britannica.com/science/child-development-process), and individual differences, as well as the areas of animal psychology, [sensation](https://www.britannica.com/topic/sensation), [perception](https://www.britannica.com/topic/perception), [memory](https://www.britannica.com/science/memory-psychology), and learning.

Training in [clinical psychology](https://www.britannica.com/science/clinical-psychology) was heavily influenced by Freudian psychology and its offshoots. But some clinical researchers, working with both normal and disturbed populations, began to develop and apply methods focusing on the learning conditions that influence and control social behaviour. This [behaviour therapy](https://www.britannica.com/science/behaviour-therapy) movement analyzed problematic behaviours (e.g., [aggressiveness](https://www.britannica.com/science/aggressive-behaviour), bizarre speech patterns, [smoking](https://www.britannica.com/topic/smoking-tobacco), fear responses) in terms of the observable events and conditions that seemed to influence the person’s problematic behaviour. Behavioral approaches led to [innovations](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/innovations) for [therapy](https://www.britannica.com/science/therapeutics) by working to modify problematic behaviour not through insight, awareness, or the uncovering of unconscious motivations but by addressing the behaviour itself. Behaviourists attempted to modify the maladaptive behaviour directly, examining the conditions controlling the individual’s current problems, not their possible historical roots. They also intended to show that such efforts could be successful without the symptom substitution that Freudian theory predicted. Freudians believed that removing the troubling behaviour directly would be followed by new and worse problems. Behaviour therapists showed that this was not necessarily the case.

To begin exploring the role of [genetics](https://www.britannica.com/science/genetics) in [personality](https://www.britannica.com/topic/personality) and [social development](https://www.britannica.com/science/social-learning), psychologists compared the similarity in personality shown by people who share the same [genes](https://www.britannica.com/science/gene) or the same [environment](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/environment). [Twin](https://www.britannica.com/science/twin) studies compared monozygotic (identical) as opposed to dizygotic (fraternal) twins, raised either in the same or in different [environments](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/environments). Overall, these studies demonstrated the important role of [heredity](https://www.britannica.com/science/heredity-genetics) in a wide range of human characteristics and traits, such as those of the [introvert and extravert](https://www.britannica.com/science/introvert), and indicated that the biological-genetic influence was far greater than early behaviourism had assumed. At the same time, it also became clear that how such [dispositions](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/dispositions) are expressed in behaviour depends importantly on interactions with the environment in the course of development, beginning in utero.
