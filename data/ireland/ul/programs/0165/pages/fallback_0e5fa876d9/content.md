Computer once meant a person who did computations, but now the term almost universally refers to automated electronic [machinery](https://www.britannica.com/technology/machine). The first section of this article focuses on modern digital electronic computers and their design, [constituent](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constituent) parts, and applications. For details on [computer architecture](https://www.britannica.com/technology/computer-architecture), [software](https://www.britannica.com/technology/software), and theory, see [computer science](https://www.britannica.com/science/computer-science).

## Computing basics

The first computers were used primarily for numerical calculations. However, as any information can be numerically encoded, people soon realized that computers are capable of general-purpose [information processing](https://www.britannica.com/technology/information-processing). Their capacity to handle large amounts of data has extended the range and accuracy of [weather forecasting](https://www.britannica.com/science/weather-forecasting). Their speed has allowed them to make decisions about routing telephone connections through a network and to [control](https://www.britannica.com/technology/control-system) mechanical systems such as automobiles, nuclear reactors, and robotic surgical tools. They are also cheap enough to be [embedded](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/embedded) in everyday appliances and to make [clothes](https://www.britannica.com/topic/dress-clothing) dryers and rice cookers “smart.” Computers have allowed us to pose and answer questions that were difficult to pursue in the past. These questions might be about [DNA](https://www.britannica.com/science/DNA) sequences in genes, patterns of activity in a consumer market, or all the uses of a word in texts that have been stored in a [database](https://www.britannica.com/technology/database). Increasingly, computers can also learn and adapt as they operate by using processes such as [machine learning](https://www.britannica.com/technology/machine-learning).

Computers also have limitations, some of which are theoretical. For example, there are undecidable propositions whose truth cannot be determined within a given set of rules, such as the logical structure of a computer. Because no universal [algorithmic](https://www.britannica.com/science/algorithm) method can exist to identify such propositions, a computer asked to obtain the truth of such a proposition will (unless forcibly interrupted) continue indefinitely—a condition known as the “[halting problem](https://www.britannica.com/topic/halting-problem).” (See [Turing machine](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Turing-machine).) Other limitations reflect current [technology](https://www.britannica.com/technology/technology). For example, although computers have progressed greatly in terms of processing data and using [artificial intelligence](https://www.britannica.com/technology/artificial-intelligence) [algorithms](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/algorithms), they are limited by their incapacity to think in a more [holistic](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/holistic) fashion. Computers may imitate humans—quite effectively, even—but imitation may not replace the human element in social interaction. [Ethical](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Ethical) concerns also limit computers, because computers rely on data, rather than a [moral](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/moral) compass or human [conscience](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/conscience), to make decisions.

[Analog](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Analog) computers use continuous physical magnitudes to represent quantitative information. At first they represented quantities with mechanical components (see [differential analyzer](https://www.britannica.com/technology/differential-analyzer) and [integrator](https://www.britannica.com/technology/integrator)), but after [World War II](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-II) voltages were used; by the 1960s digital computers had largely replaced them. Nonetheless, analog computers, and some hybrid digital-analog systems, continued in use through the 1960s in tasks such as aircraft and spaceflight simulation.

One advantage of analog computation is that it may be relatively simple to design and build an analog computer to solve a single problem. Another advantage is that analog computers can frequently represent and solve a problem in “real time”; that is, the computation proceeds at the same rate as the system being modeled by it. Their main disadvantages are that analog representations are limited in precision—typically a few decimal places but fewer in complex mechanisms—and general-purpose devices are expensive and not easily programmed.

In contrast to analog computers, digital computers represent information in [discrete](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/discrete) form, generally as sequences of 0s and 1s ([binary](https://www.britannica.com/science/binary-number-system) digits, or bits). The modern era of digital computers began in the late 1930s and early 1940s in the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States), Britain, and [Germany](https://www.britannica.com/place/Germany). The first devices used switches operated by [electromagnets](https://www.britannica.com/science/electromagnet) (relays). Their programs were stored on punched paper tape or cards, and they had limited internal data storage. For historical developments, see the section [Invention of the modern computer](https://www.britannica.com/technology/computer/Early-business-machines#ref216032).

During the 1950s and ’60s, Unisys (maker of the [UNIVAC](https://www.britannica.com/technology/UNIVAC) computer), [International Business Machines Corporation](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation) (IBM), and other companies made large, expensive computers of increasing [power](https://www.britannica.com/science/power-physics). They were used by major corporations and government research laboratories, typically as the sole computer in the organization. In 1959 the [IBM 1401](https://www.britannica.com/technology/IBM-1401) computer rented for $8,000 per month (early IBM machines were almost always leased rather than sold), and in 1964 the largest IBM S/360 computer cost several million dollars.

These computers came to be called mainframes, though the term did not become common until smaller computers were built. Mainframe computers were characterized by having (for their time) large storage capabilities, fast components, and powerful computational abilities. They were highly reliable, and, because they frequently served vital needs in an organization, they were sometimes designed with [redundant](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/redundant) components that let them survive partial failures. Because they were complex systems, they were operated by a staff of systems programmers, who alone had access to the computer. Other users submitted “batch jobs” to be run one at a time on the mainframe.

Such systems remain important today, though they are no longer the sole, or even primary, central computing resource of an organization, which will typically have hundreds or thousands of [personal computers](https://www.britannica.com/technology/personal-computer) (PCs). Mainframes now provide high-capacity data storage for [Internet](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Internet) servers, or, through [time-sharing](https://www.britannica.com/technology/time-sharing) techniques, they allow hundreds or thousands of users to run programs simultaneously. Because of their current roles, these computers are now called [servers](https://www.britannica.com/technology/server) rather than mainframes.
