A personal computer (PC), or simply computer, is a [computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer) designed for personal use. [It is typically used for tasks such as](#cite_note-1) [word processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_processor), [web browsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_browser), [email](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Email), [file management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_manager), [spreadsheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spreadsheet), and [video calling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_calling). Personal computers are meant to be operated directly by an [end user](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_user), rather than by a computer expert, administrator, company or [technician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technician). Unlike large, costly [minicomputers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minicomputer) and [mainframes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainframe), [time-sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-sharing) by many people at the same time is not used with personal computers. The term "[home computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_computer)" has also been used, primarily in the late 1970s and 1980s. The advent of personal computers and the concurrent [Digital Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Revolution) have significantly affected the lives of people.

Institutional or corporate computer owners in the 1960s had to write their own programs to do any useful work with computers. While personal computer users may develop their own applications, these systems most frequently run [commercial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_software) or free-of-charge software ("[freeware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeware)"), which commonly has [closed source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proprietary_software) (proprietary) codebases; or [free and open-source software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_and_open-source_software), where the source code is made freely available. Software for end-users is typically provided in [binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_code) (ready-to-run) form. Software for personal computers is typically developed and distributed independently from the hardware or [operating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_system) manufacturers. [Practically all personal computer users no longer need to create programs themselves to make any use of a personal computer, although end-user programming is still feasible. This contrasts with mobile systems, where software is often available only through a manufacturer-supported channel](#cite_note-2) [and end-user program development may be discouraged by lack of support by the manufacturer.](#cite_note-3)

Since the early 1990s, [Microsoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft) operating systems (first with [MS-DOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MS-DOS) and then with [Windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Windows)) and CPUs based on [Intel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel)'s [x86](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X86) architecture – collectively called [Wintel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wintel) – have dominated the personal computer market, and today the term PC normally refers to the ubiquitous Wintel platform, or to Windows PCs in general (including those running ARM chips), to the point where software for Windows is marketed as "for PC".[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)] Alternatives to Windows occupy a minority share of the market; these include the [Mac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mac_(computer)) platform from [Apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Inc.) (running the [macOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacOS) operating system), and [free and open-source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_and_open-source), [Unix-like](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unix-like) operating systems, such as [Linux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux) (including the Linux-derived [ChromeOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ChromeOS)). Other notable platforms until the 1990s were the [Amiga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiga), [Atari ST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atari_ST), and the [PC-98](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PC-98) line.

The term 'PC' is an [initialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initialism) for 'personal computer'. While the [IBM Personal Computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Personal_Computer) incorporated the designation into its model name, the term originally described personal computers of any brand. In some contexts, PC is used to contrast with the [Mac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mac_(computer)) computer.

Since none of these Apple products were mainframes or time-sharing systems, they were all personal computers but not PC (brand) computers. In 1995, a CBS segment on the growing popularity of PC reported: "For many newcomers PC stands for Pain and Confusion."

> The "brain" [computer] may one day come down to our level [of the common people] and help with our income-tax and book-keeping calculations. But this is speculation and there is no sign of it so far.

—British newspaper The Star in a June 1949 news article about the [EDSAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EDSAC) computer, long before the era of the personal computers.

In the [history of computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_computing), early experimental machines could be operated by a single attendant. For example, [ENIAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENIAC) which became operational in 1946 could be run by a single, albeit highly trained, person. [This mode pre-dated the batch programming, or time-sharing modes with multiple users connected through](#cite_note-11) [terminals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_terminal) to mainframe computers. Computers intended for laboratory, instrumentation, or engineering purposes were built, and could be operated by one person in an interactive fashion. Examples include such systems as the [Bendix G15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bendix_G15) and [LGP-30](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGP-30) of 1956, and the Soviet [MIR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIR_(computer)) series of computers developed from 1965 to 1969.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)] By the early 1970s, people in academic or research institutions had the opportunity for single-person use of a [computer system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LINC) in interactive mode for extended durations, although these systems would still have been too expensive to be owned by a single person.

The personal computer was made possible by major advances in [semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor) technology. In 1959, the [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon) [integrated circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit) (IC) chip was developed by [Robert Noyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Noyce) at [Fairchild Semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairchild_Semiconductor), [and the](#cite_note-12) [metal–oxide–semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal–oxide–semiconductor) (MOS) transistor was developed by [Mohamed Atalla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohamed_Atalla) and [Dawon Kahng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dawon_Kahng) at [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs). [The MOS integrated circuit was commercialized by](#cite_note-13) [RCA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RCA_Corporation) in 1964, [and then the](#cite_note-14) [silicon-gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon-gate) MOS integrated circuit was developed by [Federico Faggin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federico_Faggin) at Fairchild in 1968. [Faggin later used silicon-gate MOS technology to develop the first single-chip](#cite_note-15) [microprocessor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microprocessor), the [Intel 4004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_4004), in 1971. [The first](#cite_note-16) [microcomputers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microcomputer), based on microprocessors, were developed during the early 1970s. Widespread commercial availability of microprocessors, from the mid-1970s onwards, made computers cheap enough for small businesses and individuals to own.

In what was later to be called [the Mother of All Demos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mother_of_All_Demos), [SRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SRI_International) researcher [Douglas Engelbart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Engelbart) in 1968 gave a preview of features that would later become staples of personal computers: [e-mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-mail), [hypertext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypertext), [word processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_processing), [video conferencing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_conferencing), and the [mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_mouse). The demonstration required technical support staff and a mainframe time-sharing computer that were far too costly for individual business use at the time.

Early personal computers‍—‌generally called microcomputers‍—‌were often sold in a [kit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_kit) form and in limited volumes, and were of interest mostly to hobbyists and technicians. Minimal programming was done with toggle switches to enter instructions, and output was provided by [front panel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Front_panel) lamps. Practical use required adding peripherals such as keyboards, [computer displays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_display), [disk drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_drive), and [printers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printer_(computing)).

[Micral N](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micral_N) was the earliest commercial, non-kit microcomputer based on a microprocessor, the [Intel 8008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_8008). It was built starting in 1972, and a few hundred units were sold. This had been preceded by the [Datapoint 2200](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datapoint_2200) in 1970, for which the Intel 8008 had been commissioned, though not accepted for use. The CPU design implemented in the Datapoint 2200 became the basis for [x86](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X86) [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instruction_set_architecture) [used in the original](#cite_note-Weidendorfer_2011-17) [IBM PC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_PC) and its descendants.

In 1973, the [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM) Los Gatos Scientific Center developed a [portable computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portable_computer) prototype called SCAMP (Special Computer APL Machine Portable) based on the [IBM PALM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_PALM_processor) processor with a [Philips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philips) compact cassette drive, small [CRT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode_ray_tube), and full function keyboard. SCAMP emulated an [IBM 1130](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_1130) minicomputer in order to run APL/1130. [In 1973,](#cite_note-www03.ibm.com-19) [APL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/APL_(programming_language)) was generally available only on mainframe computers, and most desktop sized microcomputers such as the [Wang 2200](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_2200) or [HP 9800](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP_9800) offered only [BASIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BASIC). Because SCAMP was the first to emulate APL/1130 performance on a portable, single user computer, [PC Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PC_Magazine) in 1983 designated SCAMP a "revolutionary concept" and "the world's first personal computer". [This seminal, single user](#cite_note-20) [portable computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portable_computer) now resides in the [Smithsonian Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithsonian_Institution), Washington, D.C.. Successful demonstrations of the 1973 SCAMP prototype led to the [IBM 5100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_5100) portable microcomputer launched in 1975 with the ability to be programmed in both APL and BASIC for engineers, analysts, statisticians, and other business problem-solvers. In the late 1960s such a machine would have been nearly as large as two desks and would have weighed about half a ton.

Another desktop portable APL machine, the [MCM/70](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MCM/70), created by [Mers Kutt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mers_Kutt)'s company Micro Computer Machines Inc., was demonstrated in 1973 and shipped in 1974. Priced between $3,500 and $9,800 depending on the configuration, the MCM/70 was the first personal desktop microcomputer. It used the [Intel 8008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_8008) processor, and featured a 51-key keyboard and a Burroughs Self-Scan [plasma display](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_display) panel that could display 32 characters per line. It ran the AVS/EASY operating system, and had the capacity for 8 kilobytes of RAM and 14 kilobytes of ROM. Users could optionally add cassette drives that provided more than 100 kilobytes of external storage.

A seminal step in personal computing was the 1973 [Xerox Alto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_Alto), developed at [Xerox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox)'s [Palo Alto Research Center (PARC)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PARC_(company)). It had a graphical user interface ([GUI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GUI)) which later served as inspiration for Apple's [Macintosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_128K), and Microsoft's [Windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows) operating system. The Alto was a demonstration project, not commercialized, as the parts were too expensive to be affordable.

Also in 1973 [Hewlett Packard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP_9800_series) introduced fully [BASIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BASIC) programmable microcomputers that fit entirely on top of a desk, including a keyboard, a small one-line display, and printer. The [Wang 2200](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_2200) microcomputer of 1973 had a full-size [cathode ray tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode_ray_tube) (CRT) and cassette tape storage. [These were generally expensive specialized computers sold for business or scientific uses.](#cite_note-23)

1974 saw the introduction of what is considered by many to be the first true personal computer, the [Altair 8800](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altair_8800) created by [Micro Instrumentation and Telemetry Systems (MITS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micro_Instrumentation_and_Telemetry_Systems). [Based on the 8-bit](#cite_note-25) [Intel 8080](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_8080) Microprocessor, [the Altair is widely recognized as the spark that ignited the](#cite_note-73_Magazine-26) [microcomputer revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microcomputer_revolution) [as the first commercially successful personal computer.](#cite_note-27) [The](#cite_note-28) [computer bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_bus) designed for the Altair was to become a [de facto standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_facto_standard) in the form of the [S-100 bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-100_bus), and the first programming language for the machine was Microsoft's founding product, [Altair BASIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altair_BASIC).

In 1976, [Steve Jobs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Jobs) and [Steve Wozniak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Wozniak) sold the [Apple I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_I) computer circuit board, which was fully prepared and contained about 30 chips. The Apple I computer differed from the other kit-style hobby computers of the era. At the request of [Paul Terrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Terrell), owner of the [Byte Shop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte_Shop), Jobs and Wozniak were given their first purchase order, for 50 Apple I computers, only if the computers were assembled and tested and not a kit computer. Terrell wanted to have computers to sell to a wide range of users, not just experienced electronics hobbyists who had the soldering skills to assemble a computer kit. The [Apple I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_I) as delivered was still technically a kit computer, as it did not have a power supply, case, or keyboard when it was delivered to the Byte Shop.

The first successfully mass-marketed personal computer to be announced was the [Commodore PET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_PET) after being revealed in January 1977. However, it was back-ordered and not available until later that year. [Three months later (April), the](#cite_note-Byte_Feb_1978-31) [Apple II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_II) (usually referred to as the Apple) was announced with the first units being shipped 10 June 1977, [and the](#cite_note-Apple_II_History-32) [TRS-80](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRS-80) from [Tandy Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tandy_Corporation) / [Tandy Radio Shack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tandy_Radio_Shack) following in August 1977, which sold over 100,000 units during its lifetime. Together, especially in the North American market, these 3 machines were referred to as the "1977 trinity". Mass-market, ready-assembled computers had arrived, and allowed a wider range of people to use computers, focusing more on software applications and less on development of the processor hardware.

In 1977 the Heath company introduced personal computer kits known as [Heathkits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathkit), starting with the [Heathkit H8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathkit_H8), followed by the Heathkit H89 in late 1979. With the purchase of the Heathkit H8 you would obtain the chassis and CPU card to assemble yourself, additional hardware such as the H8-1 memory board that contained 4k of RAM could also be purchased in order to run software. The Heathkit H11 model was released in 1978 and was one of the first 16-bit personal computers; however, due to its high retail cost of $1,295 was discontinued in 1982.

During the early 1980s, [home computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_computer) were further developed for household use, with software for personal productivity, programming and games. They typically could be used with a television already in the home as the computer display, with low-detail blocky graphics and a limited color range, and text about 40 characters wide by 25 characters tall. [Sinclair Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinclair_Research), [a UK company, produced the ZX Series‍—‌the](#cite_note-Sinclair_Research-36) [ZX80](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZX80) (1980), [ZX81](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZX81) (1981), and the [ZX Spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZX_Spectrum); the latter was introduced in 1982, and totaled 8 million unit sold. Following came the [Commodore 64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_64), totaled 17 million units sold, [the](#cite_note-Reimer2-38) [Galaksija](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaksija_(computer)) (1983) [introduced in](#cite_note-39) [Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yugoslavia) and the [Amstrad CPC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amstrad_CPC) series (464–6128).

In the same year, the [NEC PC-98](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NEC_PC-98) was introduced, which was a very popular personal computer that sold more than 18 million units. [Another famous personal computer, the revolutionary](#cite_note-40) [Amiga 1000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiga_1000), was unveiled by [Commodore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_International) on 23 July 1985. The Amiga 1000 featured a multitasking, windowing operating system, color graphics with a 4096-color palette, stereo sound, Motorola 68000 CPU, 256KB RAM, and 880KB 3.5-inch disk drive, for US$1,295.

IBM's first PC, the [IBM 5150](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_5150), was introduced on 12 August 1981 [at a price of US$1,565. It operated with the 5 megahertz (later 16 megahertz)](#cite_note-42) [Intel 8088](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_8088) chip. What was later dubbed [MS-DOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MS-DOS) was the computer's Microsoft-produced operating system. A word processor [EasyWriter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EasyWriter) and a spreadsheet client [VisiCalc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VisiCalc) were preinstalled. Customers could pay an additional $3,000 to add a printer, a display and two diskette drives. The 5150's [open architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_architecture) (IBM encouraged the development of third-party software and peripherals for the computer) set a mass market standard for PC design.

In 1982 the personal computer, dubbed The Computer, was named [Machine of the Year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_Person_of_the_Year) by [Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_(magazine)) magazine.

Somewhat larger and more expensive systems were aimed at office and small business use. These often featured 80-column text displays but might not have had graphics or sound capabilities. These microprocessor-based systems were still less costly than time-shared mainframes or minicomputers.

[Workstations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workstation) were characterized by high-performance processors and graphics displays, with large-capacity local disk storage, networking capability, and running under a [multitasking operating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multitasking_operating_system). Eventually, due to the [influence of the IBM PC on the personal computer market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influence_of_the_IBM_PC_on_the_personal_computer_market), personal computers and home computers lost any technical distinction. Business computers acquired color graphics capability and sound, and home computers and game systems users used the same processors and operating systems as office workers. Mass-market computers had graphics capabilities and memory comparable to dedicated workstations of a few years before. Even local area networking, originally a way to allow business computers to share expensive mass storage and peripherals, became a standard feature of personal computers used at home.

An increasingly important set of uses for personal computers relied on the ability of the computer to communicate with other computer systems, allowing interchange of information. Experimental public access to a shared mainframe computer system was demonstrated as early as 1973 in the [Community Memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_Memory) project, but [bulletin board systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulletin_board_system) and [online service providers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_service_provider) became more commonly available after 1978. Commercial [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet) service providers emerged in the late 1980s, giving public access to the rapidly growing network.

In 1984, [Apple Computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Computer) launched the [Macintosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_128K), with an [advertisement during the Super Bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_(advertisement)). The Macintosh was the first successful mass-market [mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mouse_(computing))-driven computer with a [graphical user interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphical_user_interface) or 'WIMP' (Windows, Icons, Menus, and Pointers). Based on the [Motorola 68000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motorola_68000) microprocessor, the Macintosh included many of the Lisa's features at a price of US$2,495. The Macintosh was introduced with 128KB of RAM and later that year a 512KB RAM model became available. To reduce costs compared to the Lisa, the year-younger Macintosh had a simplified motherboard design, no internal hard drive, and a single 3.5-inch floppy drive. Applications that came with the Macintosh included [MacPaint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacPaint), a bit-mapped graphics program, and [MacWrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacWrite), which demonstrated [WYSIWYG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WYSIWYG) word processing.

The Macintosh was a successful personal computer for years to come. This is particularly due to the introduction of [desktop publishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desktop_publishing) in 1985 through Apple's partnership with [Adobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_Systems). This partnership introduced the [LaserWriter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LaserWriter) printer and [Aldus PageMaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldus_PageMaker) to users of the personal computer. During [Steve Jobs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Jobs)'s hiatus from Apple, a number of different models of Macintosh, including the [Macintosh Plus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_Plus) and [Macintosh II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_II), were released to a great degree of success. The entire Macintosh line of computers was IBM's major competition up until the early 1990s.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

In 1991, the [World Wide Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web) was made available for public use. The combination of powerful personal computers with high-resolution graphics and sound, with the infrastructure provided by the Internet, and the standardization of access methods of the [Web browsers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_browser), established the foundation for a significant fraction of modern life, from bus time tables through unlimited distribution of free videos through to online user-edited encyclopedias.

A workstation is a high-end personal computer designed for technical, mathematical, or scientific applications. Intended primarily to be used by one person at a time, they are commonly connected to a [local area network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_area_network) and run multi-user [operating systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_system). Workstations are used for tasks such as [computer-aided design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-aided_design), drafting and modeling, computation-intensive scientific and engineering calculations, image processing, [architectural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architectural) modeling, and [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics) for animation and motion picture visual effects.

Before the widespread use of PCs, a computer that could fit on a [desk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desk) was remarkably small, leading to the desktop nomenclature. More recently, the phrase usually indicates a particular style of [computer case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_case). Desktop computers come in a variety of styles ranging from large vertical [tower cases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_case) to small models which can be tucked behind or rest directly beneath (and support) [LCD monitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LCD_monitor).

While the term desktop often refers to a computer with a vertically aligned computer [tower case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_case), these varieties often rest on the ground or underneath desks. Despite this seeming contradiction, the term desktop does typically refer to these vertical tower cases as well as the horizontally aligned models which are designed to literally rest on top of desks and are therefore more appropriate to the desktop term, although both types qualify for this desktop label in most practical situations aside from certain physical arrangement differences. Both styles of these computer cases hold the systems hardware [components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_hardware) such as the [motherboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherboard), processor chip and other internal operating parts. Desktop computers have an external monitor with a display screen and an external keyboard, which are plugged into ports on the back of the computer case. Desktop computers are popular for home and business computing applications as they leave space on the desk for [multiple monitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_monitors).

A [gaming computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaming_computer) is a desktop computer that generally comprises a high-performance [video card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_card), processor and RAM, to improve the speed and responsiveness of demanding [video games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PC_game).

An [all-in-one computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-in-one_computer) (also known as single-unit PCs) is a desktop computer that combines the monitor and systems hardware components within a single unit. A separate keyboard and mouse are standard input devices, with some monitors including [touchscreen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Touchscreen) capability. The processor and other working components are typically reduced in size relative to standard desktops, located behind the monitor, and configured similarly to laptops.

A [nettop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nettop) computer was introduced by [Intel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel) in February 2008, characterized by low cost and lean functionality. These were intended to be used with an Internet connection to run [Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web) browsers and Internet applications.

A [home theater PC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_theater_PC) (HTPC) combines the functions of a personal computer and a [digital video recorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_video_recorder). It is connected to a [TV set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV_set) or an appropriately sized [computer display](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_display), and is often used as a digital photo viewer, music and video player, TV receiver, and digital video recorder. HTPCs are also referred to as media center systems or [media servers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_server). The goal is to combine many or all components of a [home theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_cinema) setup into one box. HTPCs can also connect to services providing on-demand movies and TV shows. HTPCs can be purchased pre-configured with the required hardware and software needed to add television programming to the PC, or can be assembled from components.

[Keyboard computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keyboard_computer) are computers inside of keyboards, generally still designed to be connected to an external [computer monitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_monitor) or [television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television). Examples include the [Atari ST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atari_ST), [Amstrad CPC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amstrad_CPC), [BBC Micro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Micro), [Commodore 64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_64), [MSX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MSX), [Raspberry Pi 400](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raspberry_Pi#Raspberry_Pi_400), and the [ZX Spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZX_Spectrum).

The potential utility of [portable computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portable_computer) was apparent early on. [Alan Kay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Kay) described the [Dynabook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynabook) in 1972, but no hardware was developed. The [Xerox NoteTaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_NoteTaker) was produced in a very small experimental batch around 1978. In 1975, the [IBM 5100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_5100) could be fit into a transport case, making it a portable computer, but it weighed about 50 pounds. Such early portable computers were termed [luggables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luggable) by journalists owing to their heft.

Before the introduction of the IBM PC, portable computers consisting of a processor, display, disk drives and keyboard, in a suit-case style portable housing, allowed users to bring a computer home from the office or to take notes at a classroom. Examples include the [Osborne 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osborne_1) and [Kaypro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaypro); and the [Commodore SX-64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_SX-64). These machines were AC-powered and included a small CRT display screen. The form factor was intended to allow these systems to be taken on board an airplane as carry-on baggage, though their high power demand meant that they could not be used in flight. The integrated CRT display made for a relatively heavy package, but these machines were more portable than their contemporary desktop equals. Some models had standard or optional connections to drive an external video monitor, allowing a larger screen or use with video projectors.

IBM PC-compatible suitcase format computers became available soon after the introduction of the PC, with the [Compaq Portable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compaq_Portable) being a leading example of the type. Later models included a [hard drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_drive) to give roughly equivalent performance to contemporary desktop computers.

The development of thin plasma display and LCD screens permitted a somewhat smaller form factor, called the lunchbox computer. The screen formed one side of the enclosure, with a detachable keyboard and one or two half-height floppy disk drives, mounted facing the ends of the computer. Some variations included a battery, allowing operation away from AC outlets.

A [laptop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laptop) computer is a type of personal computer designed for portability via its [clamshell design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clamshell_design), where the keyboard and computer components are on one panel, with a hinged second panel containing a flat display screen. Closing the laptop protects the screen and keyboard during transportation. Laptops generally have a rechargeable [battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_(electricity)), enhancing their portability. To save power, weight and space, laptop graphics chips are in many cases integrated into the CPU or chipset and use system RAM, resulting in reduced graphics performance when compared to desktop machines, that more typically have a graphics card installed. For this reason, desktop computers are usually preferred over laptops for [gaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game) purposes.

Unlike desktop computers, only minor internal upgrades (such as memory and hard disk drive) are feasible owing to the limited space and power available. Laptops have the same input and output ports as desktops, for connecting to external displays, mice, cameras, storage devices and keyboards. Laptops are also a little more expensive compared to desktops, as the miniaturized components for laptops themselves are expensive.

[Notebook computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notebook_computer) such as the [TRS-80 Model 100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRS-80_Model_100) and [Epson HX-20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epson_HX-20) had roughly the plan dimensions of a sheet of typing paper ([ANSI A or ISO A4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_size)). These machines had a keyboard with slightly reduced dimensions compared to a desktop system, and a fixed LCD screen coplanar with the keyboard. These displays were usually small, with 8 to 16 lines of text, sometimes only 40 columns line length. However, these machines could operate for extended times on disposable or rechargeable batteries. Although they did not usually include internal disk drives, this form factor often included a [modem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modem) for telephone communication and often had provisions for external cassette or disk storage. Later, clamshell format laptop computers with similar small plan dimensions were also called notebooks.[:16](#cite_note-fd-48)

A [desktop replacement computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desktop_replacement_computer) is a portable computer that provides the full capabilities of a [desktop computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desktop_computer). Such computers are currently large laptops. This class of computers usually includes more powerful components and a larger display than generally found in smaller portable computers, and may have limited battery capacity or no battery.

[Netbooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netbook), also called mini notebooks or [subnotebooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subnotebook), were a subgroup of laptops [suited for general computing tasks and accessing](#cite_note-cnnogg-51) [web-based applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_application). Initially, the primary defining characteristic of netbooks was the lack of an [optical disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_disc) drive, smaller size, and lower performance than full-size laptops. By mid-2009 netbooks had been offered to users "free of charge", with an extended service contract purchase of a cellular data plan.[Ultrabooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrabook) and [Chromebooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromebook) have since filled the gap left by Netbooks. Unlike the generic Netbook name, Ultrabook and Chromebook are technically both specifications by Intel and Google respectively. Networks fell out of popularity during the early 2010s as more mobile devices, such as [smartphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smartphones) and [tablets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tablet_computer) became more accessible and powerful.

A [tablet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tablet_computer) uses a [touchscreen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Touchscreen) display, which can be controlled using either a [stylus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stylus) pen or finger. Some tablets may use a hybrid or convertible design, offering a keyboard that can either be removed as an attachment, or a screen that can be rotated and folded directly over top the keyboard. Some tablets may use desktop-PC operating system such as Windows or Linux, or may run an operating system designed primarily for tablets. Many tablet computers have USB ports, to which a keyboard or mouse can be connected.

[Smartphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smartphone) are often similar to [tablet computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tablet_computer), the difference being that smartphones always have [cellular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_frequencies) integration. They are generally smaller than tablets, and may not have a slate form factor.

The ultra-mobile PC (UMPC) is a small [tablet computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tablet_computer). It was developed by [Microsoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft), [Intel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel) and [Samsung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samsung_Electronics), among others. Current UMPCs typically feature the Windows XP, Windows Vista, Windows 7, or Linux [operating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_system), and low-voltage [Intel Atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_Atom) or [VIA C7-M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VIA_C7-M) processors.

A pocket PC is a hardware specification for a handheld-sized computer ([personal digital assistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_digital_assistant), PDA) that runs the [Microsoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft) [Windows Mobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Mobile) [operating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_system). It may have the capability to run an alternative [operating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_system) like [NetBSD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NetBSD) or [Linux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux). Pocket PCs have many of the capabilities of desktop [PCs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_PC_compatible). Numerous [applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software) are available for handhelds adhering to the Microsoft Pocket PC specification, many of which are [freeware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeware). Microsoft-compliant Pocket PCs can also be used with many other add-ons like [GPS receivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System), [barcode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barcode) readers, [RFID](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RFID) readers and cameras.

In 2007, with the release of Windows Mobile 6, Microsoft dropped the name Pocket PC in favor of a new naming scheme: devices without an integrated phone are called Windows Mobile Classic instead of Pocket PC, while devices with an integrated phone and a touch screen are called Windows Mobile Professional.

[Palmtop PCs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palmtop_PC) were miniature pocket-sized computers running [MS-DOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MS-DOS) that first came about in the late 1980s, typically in a clamshell form factor with a keyboard. Non-x86 based devices were often called palmtop computers, examples being [Psion Series 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psion_Series_3). In later years a hardware specification called [Handheld PC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handheld_PC) was later released by Microsoft that run the [Windows CE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_CE) operating system.

[Computer hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_hardware) is a comprehensive term for all physical and tangible parts of a computer, as distinguished from the data it contains or operates on, and the software that provides instructions for the hardware to accomplish tasks. Some sub-systems of a personal computer may contain processors that run a fixed program, or [firmware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firmware), such as a keyboard controller. Firmware usually is not changed by the end user of the personal computer.

Most 2010s and 2020s-era personal computers require users only to plug in the power supply, monitor, and other cables. A typical [desktop computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desktop_computer) consists of a [computer case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_case) (or tower), a metal chassis that holds the [power supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_power_supply), [motherboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherboard), a storage device such as a [hard disk drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive) or [solid-state drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_drive), and often an [optical disc drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_disc_drive). Most towers have empty space where users can add additional components. External devices such as a [computer monitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_monitor) or [visual display unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_display_unit), [keyboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_keyboard), and a [pointing device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointing_device) ([mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_mouse)) are usually found in a personal computer.

The [motherboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherboard) connects all processor, memory and peripheral devices together. The [RAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAM), graphics card and processor are in most cases mounted directly onto the motherboard. The [central processing unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_processing_unit) (microprocessor chip) plugs into a [CPU socket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPU_socket), while the ram modules plug into corresponding ram sockets. Some motherboards have the video display adapter, sound and other peripherals integrated onto the motherboard, while others use [expansion slots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expansion_slot) for graphics cards, network cards, or other [input/output](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Input/output) devices. The graphics card or sound card may employ a [break out box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Break_out_box) to keep the analog parts away from the [electromagnetic radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation) inside the computer case. Disk drives, which provide mass storage, are connected to the motherboard with one cable, and to the power supply through another cable. Usually, disk drives are mounted in the same case as the motherboard; expansion chassis are also made for additional disk storage.

For large amounts of data, a [tape drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tape_drive) can be used or extra hard disks can be put together in an external case. The keyboard and the mouse are external devices plugged into the computer through connectors on an I/O panel on the back of the computer case. The monitor is also connected to the input/output (I/O) panel, either through an onboard port on the motherboard, or a port on the graphics card. Capabilities of the personal computer's hardware can sometimes be extended by the addition of [expansion cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expansion_card) connected via an expansion [bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_bus). Standard peripheral buses often used for adding expansion cards in personal computers include [PCI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripheral_Component_Interconnect), [PCI Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PCI_Express) (PCIe), and [AGP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerated_Graphics_Port) (a high-speed PCI bus dedicated to graphics adapters, found in older computers). Most modern personal computers have multiple physical PCI Express expansion slots, with some having PCI slots as well.

A [peripheral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripheral) is a device connected to a computer to provide communication (such as input and output) or auxiliary functions (such as additional storage). [Peripherals generally connect to the computer through the use of USB ports or inputs located on the I/O panel.](#cite_note-54) [USB flash drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB_flash_drive) provide portable storage using flash memory which allows users to access the files stored on the drive on any computer. [Memory cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_card) also provide portable storage for users, commonly used on other electronics such as mobile phones and digital cameras, the information stored on these cards can be accessed using a memory card reader to transfer data between devices. Webcams, which are either built into computer hardware or connected via USB are video cameras that records video in real time to either be saved to the computer or streamed somewhere else over the internet. [Game controllers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_controller) can be plugged in via USB and can be used as an input device for video games as an alternative to using keyboard and mouse. [Headphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headphones) and speakers can be connected via USB or through an auxiliary port (found on I/O panel) and allow users to listen to audio accessed on their computer; however, speakers may also require an additional power source to operate. [Microphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microphone) can be connected through an audio input port on the I/O panel and allow the computer to convert sound into an electrical signal to be used or transmitted by the computer.

Computer software is any kind of [computer program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_program), [procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithm), or documentation that performs some task on a computer system. [The term includes](#cite_note-55) [application software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Application_software) such as [word processors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_processor) that perform productive tasks for users, [system software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_software) such as [operating systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_system) that interface with [computer hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_hardware) to provide the necessary services for application software, and [middleware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middleware) that controls and co-ordinates [distributed systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_systems).

Software applications are common for [word processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_processing), [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet) browsing, [Internet faxing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_fax), [e-mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-mail) and other digital messaging, [multimedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multimedia) playback, playing of [computer game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game), and [computer programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_programming). The user may have significant knowledge of the operating environment and application programs, but is not necessarily interested in programming nor even able to write programs for the computer. Therefore, most [software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software) written primarily for personal computers tends to be designed with simplicity of use, or [user-friendliness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User-friendliness) in mind. However, the [software industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_industry) continuously provide a wide range of new products for use in personal computers, targeted at both the expert and the non-expert user.

An operating system (OS) manages computer resources and provides programmers with an [interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_interface) used to access those resources. An operating system processes system data and user input, and responds by allocating and managing tasks and internal system resources as a service to users and programs of the system. An operating system performs basic tasks such as controlling and allocating [memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_data_storage), prioritizing system requests, controlling [input/output](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Input/output) devices, facilitating [computer networking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network), and managing files.

Common contemporary desktop operating systems are [Microsoft Windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Windows), [macOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacOS), [Linux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux), [Solaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solaris_(operating_system)) and [FreeBSD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FreeBSD). Windows, macOS, and Linux all have server and personal variants. With the exception of Microsoft Windows, the designs of each of them were inspired by or directly inherited from the [Unix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unix) operating system.

Early personal computers used operating systems that supported [command line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Command_line) interaction, using an alphanumeric display and keyboard. The user had to remember a large range of commands to, for example, open a file for editing or to move text from one place to another. Starting in the early 1960s, the advantages of a [graphical user interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphical_user_interface) began to be explored, but widespread adoption required lower-cost graphical display equipment. By 1984, mass-market computer systems using graphical user interfaces were available; by the turn of the 21st century, text-mode operating systems were no longer a significant fraction of the personal computer market.

Generally, a computer user uses application software to carry out a specific task.[System software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_software) supports applications [and provides common services such as memory management, network connectivity and device drivers, all of which may be used by applications but are not directly of interest to the end user. A simplified](#cite_note-Weik_2000-57) [analogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogy) in the world of hardware would be the relationship of an electric light bulb (an application) to an electric power generation plant (a system): [the power plant merely generates electricity, not itself of any real use until harnessed to an application like the electric light that performs a service that benefits the user.](#cite_note-58)

Typical examples of software applications are [word processors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_processor), [spreadsheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spreadsheet), and [media players](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_player_(application_software)). Multiple applications bundled together as a package are sometimes referred to as an application suite. [Microsoft Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Office) and [LibreOffice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LibreOffice), [which bundle together a word processor, a spreadsheet, and several other discrete applications, are typical examples.](#cite_note-59) [The separate applications in a suite usually have a](#cite_note-60) [user interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_interface) that has some commonality making it easier for the user to learn and use each application. Often, they may have some capability to interact with each other in ways beneficial to the user; for example, a spreadsheet might be able to be embedded in a word processor document even though it had been created in the separate spreadsheet application.

[End-user development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End-user_development) tailors systems to meet the user's specific needs. User-written software include spreadsheet templates, word processor macros, scientific simulations, graphics and animation scripts; even email filters are a kind of user software. Users create this software themselves and often overlook how important it is.

[According to IDC, in 2013 PC shipments dropped by 9.8% as the greatest drop-ever in line with consumers trends to use mobile devices.](#cite_note-88)

In the second quarter of 2018, PC sales grew for the first time since the first quarter of 2012. According to research firm Gartner, the growth mainly came from the business market while the consumer market experienced decline.

In 2020, as the result of the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic) with more people working at home and learning remotely, PC sales grew by 26.1% compared to previous years according to [IDC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Data_Group). [According to Canalys, 2020 was the highest growth rate for the PC market since 2011.](#cite_note-91)

This upward trend continued into 2021, with global PC shipments reaching over 340 million units, the highest since 2012, representing a 14.6% year-over-year growth.[Notebooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notebook_computer) and mobile workstations led the charge, with shipments growing 16% to reach 275 million units, while desktops and desktop workstations increased 7% to reach 66 million units. [Major vendors such as](#cite_note-94) [Lenovo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenovo), [HP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP_Inc.), [Dell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dell), and [Apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Inc.) all saw significant increases in sales.

The market saw increased demand not only from consumers and students but also from businesses investing in hybrid work infrastructure. However, by late 2022, the market began to stabilize as demand cooled down and supply chain challenges, including chip shortages, began to ease. Worldwide shipments of desktop and laptop computers fell by 19.5% in the third quarter of 2022 compared with the year-ago period, marking the steepest decline Gartner has documented in more than two decades of tracking the market.

After a period of volatility, the global PC market began to stabilize in 2023. According to IDC, worldwide PC shipments during the fourth quarter of 2024 grew 1.8% year-over-year, reaching 68.9 million units. [Canalys reported a 3.2% annual growth in the first quarter of 2024, totaling 57.2 million units, with notebook shipments increasing by 4.2%.](#cite_note-97)

In the first quarter of 2025, global PC shipments experienced a significant uptick, growing 9.4% year-over-year to 62.7 million units. This surge was partly attributed to manufacturers accelerating shipments to the U.S. ahead of newly implemented tariffs under President Donald Trump's trade policies. Lenovo maintained its lead in the global PC market, shipping 15.2 million units with an 11% growth, followed by HP with 12.8 million units (6% growth), Dell with 9.5 million units (3% growth), and Apple with 6.5 million units, marking a 22% increase. [The integration of](#cite_note-99) [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence) (AI) capabilities into PCs emerged as a significant trend during this period.

Selling prices of personal computers steadily declined due to lower costs of production and manufacture, while the capabilities of computers increased. In 1975, an Altair kit sold for around only US$400, but required customers to solder components into circuit boards; peripherals required to interact with the system in alphanumeric form instead of blinking lights would add another $2,000, and the resultant system was of use only to hobbyists.

At their introduction in 1981, the US$1,795 price of the [Osborne 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osborne_1) and its competitor [Kaypro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaypro) was considered an attractive price point; these systems had text-only displays and only floppy disks for storage. By 1982, [Michael Dell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Dell) observed that a personal computer system selling at retail for about US$3,000 was made of components that cost the dealer about $600; typical gross margin on a computer unit was around $1,000. [The total value of personal computer purchases in the US in 1983 was about $4 billion. By late 1998, the average selling price of personal computer systems in the United States had dropped below $1,000.](#cite_note-101)

For [Microsoft Windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Windows) systems, the [average selling price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average_selling_price) (ASP) showed a decline in 2008/2009, possibly due to low-cost [netbooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netbook), drawing $569 for [desktop computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desktop_computer) and $689 for [laptops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laptop) at U.S. retail in August 2008. In 2009, ASP had further fallen to $533 for desktops and to $602 for notebooks by January and to $540 and 560 in February. [According to research firm](#cite_note-103) [NPD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NPD_Group), the average selling price of all Windows portable PCs has fallen from $659 in October 2008 to $519 in October 2009.

External costs of environmental impact are not fully included in the selling price of personal computers.

Personal computers have become a large contributor to the 50 million tons of discarded electronic waste generated annually, according to the [United Nations Environment Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Environment_Programme). To address the [electronic waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_waste) issue affecting developing countries and the environment, [extended producer responsibility (EPR)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_producer_responsibility) acts have been implemented in various countries and states. [In the absence of comprehensive national legislation or regulation on the export and import of electronic waste, the](#cite_note-106) [Silicon Valley Toxics Coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Valley_Toxics_Coalition) and BAN (Basel Action Network) teamed up with electronic recyclers in the US and Canada to create an e-steward program for the orderly disposal of electronic waste. [Some organizations [](#cite_note-bannews-107)[who?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Unsupported_attributions)] oppose EPR regulation, and claim that manufacturers naturally move toward reduced material and energy use.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]
